Unit 1 · Foundations of Nursing Care for Adult Clients · Chapter 1

Health, Wellness, and Illness

Health and wellness are individualized and dynamic. In this chapter, the nurse connects a client's usual functioning, modifiable risks, environment, health beliefs, and support systems to practical health-promotion planning.

TL;DR - Wellness Is Individualized

Do not judge wellness by diagnosis alone. Compare the client with the client's usual baseline, identify modifiable risks, assess barriers to adherence, and build health education around the client's beliefs, resources, and support system.

Priority Lens

  • Health and wellness include physical function plus emotional and social contentment.
  • Illness is the client's altered functioning; disease is the physiologic alteration.
  • The illness-wellness continuum centers on the client's normal state of health.

Common Test Move

  • Choose interventions that strengthen coping, activate support, improve education, and reduce barriers.
  • Classify risks as modifiable or nonmodifiable before planning education.
  • Look for denial, mistrust, cultural mismatch, finances, or weak support as adherence barriers.

High-Yield Framework

Variables That Shape Wellness

Assess first

Modifiable

  • Tobacco or other health-risk behaviors.
  • Nutrition, weight, activity, and exercise patterns.
  • Access to health education and preventive care.
  • Sexual practices and other behavior-linked risks.

Nonmodifiable

  • Age, sex, developmental level, and genetic traits.
  • Some inherited or lifelong conditions.
  • Past life experiences that influence current beliefs.
  • Current baseline when a chronic condition is stable.
NCLEX habit: education works best when it targets what the client can actually change. Do not spend the priority intervention on a fixed trait when a modifiable risk is present.

Health/Wellness Assessment

Adherence

ATI frames health promotion around assessment plus barrier reduction. Before teaching, determine what the client believes, what resources exist, and what is blocking follow-through.

  • Complete a focused physical assessment and compare findings with the client's usual state.
  • Ask how the client perceives the illness, its severity, and the prescribed plan.
  • Identify access barriers: cost, transportation, health care availability, and education needs.
  • Assess trust in the provider, belief in the therapy, cultural or religious fit, family roles, and support systems.
  • Look for financial restrictions that can force the client to prioritize one health need over another.

Nursing Actions That Promote Wellness

Intervene
  • Provide resources that strengthen coping, such as dietitian referral, support groups, or practical home tools.
  • With permission, involve the client's support system during illness and stress.
  • Identify obstacles to health and create realistic strategies to reduce them.
  • Use education methods that increase awareness and reduce risk.
  • Improve adherence with concrete supports: written materials, medication organizers, and follow-up planning.

Emergency Nursing Principles and Management

Emergency nursing starts with rapid recognition, triage, and a systematic primary survey. The nurse prioritizes life-threatening problems first, communicates deterioration early, and follows emergency protocols for toxic, thermal, and cardiac events.

TL;DR - ABCDE Before Everything Else

Use triage to sort urgency, then use the ABCDE primary survey to find threats to life: airway/cervical spine, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure/environment. Call rapid response for signs of sudden decline and follow BLS/ACLS-based protocols for arrest rhythms.

Immediate Priorities

  • Airway is first; without oxygenation, the rest of the survey cannot help.
  • Use jaw-thrust when trauma or cervical spine injury is suspected.
  • Control visible bleeding, obtain large-bore IV access, and replace volume as ordered.

Exam Traps

  • Do not induce vomiting or use ipecac for ingested toxins.
  • Asystole is not a shockable rhythm; prioritize CPR, IV access, epinephrine per protocol, and reversible causes.
  • Use SBAR when activating or documenting a rapid response event.

Thermal and Toxic Emergencies

Rapid support

Cold/heat exposure

  • Frostbite can require rewarming in warm water and tetanus protection.
  • Prevent or treat hypothermia by removing wet clothing, warming the room, using warm blankets, and infusing warmed fluids as prescribed.
  • Heat stroke is an emergency: prioritize ABCDE, oxygen as needed, large-gauge IV access, cooling measures, and urine output monitoring.

Poisoning

  • Identify the agent, provide supportive care, stop absorption, remove the toxin when appropriate, and administer antidotes when ordered.
  • For ingestion, ATI supports activated charcoal, gastric lavage with aspiration when appropriate, and aspiration procedures; ipecac is not recommended.
  • Use naloxone for opioid toxicity and benzodiazepine therapy for seizures when prescribed.

Rapid Response and Cardiac Emergency

Escalate
  • Activate the rapid response team for early signs of sudden decline before arrest or stroke occurs.
  • Expect critical-care support such as an ICU nurse, respiratory therapist, provider, or hospitalist based on facility policy.
  • Use SBAR to communicate and document the event.
  • For ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, begin BLS components and defibrillate according to protocol.
  • For PEA or asystole, begin BLS components, establish IV access, administer epinephrine per protocol, and consider reversible causes.
PEA reversible causes: think Hs and Ts - hypovolemia, hypoxia, acidosis, potassium imbalance, hypothermia, toxins, tamponade, tension pneumothorax, coronary thrombosis, and pulmonary thrombosis.

Neurologic Diagnostic Procedures

Neurologic diagnostic care is about matching the test to the risk: contrast, bleeding, sedation, metal, infection, intracranial pressure, or postprocedure CSF leak. The nurse protects the client before, during, and after the scan or procedure.

TL;DR - Know the Test Risk

For neuro diagnostics, ask: Is contrast used? Is the client pregnant or allergic? Is kidney function adequate? Is bleeding risk present? Is metal or claustrophobia a concern? Does the procedure enter the CNS or dura? Those answers drive the nursing priorities.

High-Yield Safety Checks

  • Contrast procedures: check allergy history and kidney function.
  • Angiography: assess bleeding risk and distal circulation after catheter removal.
  • MRI: remove metal, screen implants, and address claustrophobia.

Priority Follow-Up

  • After lumbar puncture, keep the client flat as prescribed and monitor for headache.
  • For ICP monitoring, preserve system integrity and sterile technique.
  • Use GCS trends to communicate neurologic change quickly.

Function, Consciousness, and Pressure

Trend changes

EEG and GCS

  • EEG assesses brain electrical activity and is commonly used for seizure activity, sleep disorders, and behavioral changes.
  • Teach the client to wash hair, avoid oils/gels/sprays, stay awake as instructed, and avoid stimulant or sedative medications if prescribed.
  • GCS reports eye, verbal, and motor response. A total below 8 is associated with severe head injury/coma; 9-12 is moderate; above 13 is minor head trauma.

ICP monitoring

  • Used for early identification and treatment of increased intracranial pressure, especially with coma or GCS 8 or lower.
  • Normal ICP is about 10-15 mm Hg; sustained elevation threatens cerebral circulation.
  • Maintain a closed/sterile system, inspect insertion site, recalibrate per policy, and monitor neurologic status and waveforms.

Lumbar Puncture

CSF care
  • Purpose: withdraw CSF for analysis, reduce CSF pressure, instill contrast/air, or administer medication/chemotherapy into spinal fluid.
  • Preprocedure: discuss risks/benefits, screen for increased ICP or bleeding risk, remove jewelry, have the client void, and position to flex the spine.
  • Postprocedure: monitor puncture site and maintain prescribed bed rest to promote clotting and reduce post-lumbar puncture headache risk.
  • If headache occurs from CSF leakage, support flat positioning, hydration, prescribed pain medication, and possible epidural blood patch if persistent.

Pain Management

Pain management begins with believing and reassessing the client's report. The nurse combines pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic strategies, uses functional goals, and watches closely for opioid sedation and respiratory depression.

TL;DR - Pain Is What the Client Says

Pain is subjective, so the client is the best source. Assess location, quality, intensity, timing, setting, associated manifestations, and aggravating/relieving factors. Treat undertreatment as a serious complication, and monitor opioids with both pain and sedation/respiratory assessments.

Pain Types

  • Acute: protective, temporary, resolves with healing.
  • Chronic: ongoing or recurrent, often longer than 3 months.
  • Nociceptive: tissue damage/inflammation; somatic or visceral.
  • Neuropathic: abnormal nerve processing; often burning, shooting, or pins-and-needles.

Safety Priorities

  • Premedicate before painful procedures when appropriate.
  • Only the client should press the PCA button unless a provider authorizes otherwise.
  • Sedation precedes respiratory depression; intervene early.

Interventions and Medication Safety

Reassess
  • Use both pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic strategies, considering the client's preferences and functional pain goal.
  • Premedicate before painful care such as repositioning, wound care, or invasive diagnostic testing when appropriate.
  • Nonpharmacologic strategies can reduce stress and coping burden: mind-body practices, cognitive approaches, distraction, and other complementary approaches chosen by the client.
  • Nonopioids are appropriate for mild to moderate pain and can be paired with opioids for more intense pain. Track total acetaminophen intake.
  • Opioids are appropriate for moderate to severe pain. Monitor constipation, orthostatic hypotension, urinary retention, nausea/vomiting, sedation, and respiratory depression.
  • PCA provides small, frequent opioid doses with a lockout interval; the client should report inadequate relief rather than waiting until pain is severe.
  • If respiratory rate is below 8/min and shallow or the client is difficult to arouse, stop the opioid, notify/act per protocol, and prepare naloxone as prescribed.

TypeDefinition/Characteristics
AcuteProtective and temporary; usually resolves with tissue healing; can produce tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety, diaphoresis, muscle tension, grimacing, guarding, or moaning.
ChronicOngoing or frequently recurring; persists beyond healing or longer than 3 months; can reduce function and contribute to fatigue or depression.

Application Exercises

1

A client has mild acute pain after an ankle sprain. Which analgesic should the nurse expect?

  • A. Ketorolac.
  • B. Ketamine.
  • C. Meperidine.
  • D. Methadone.
A Ketorolac is an NSAID and fits mild acute inflammatory pain after a sprain.
2

A client with cancer takes extended-release opioids twice daily and reports an increase in localized aching over the last few days. How should the nurse document this increase?

  • A. Phantom limb pain.
  • B. Mixed pain.
  • C. Breakthrough pain.
  • D. Neuropathic pain.
C Breakthrough pain is an acute flare above the client's usual or baseline pain level.
3

A client is using morphine by PCA after abdominal surgery. Which statement shows correct understanding?

  • A. "I'll wait to use the device until it's absolutely necessary."
  • B. "I'll be careful about pushing the button so I don't get an overdose."
  • C. "I should tell the nurse if the pain doesn't stop after I use this device."
  • D. "I will ask my son to push the dose button when I am sleeping."
C The client should report inadequate pain control so the plan can be reevaluated.
D The client should be the one to press the PCA button unless a provider authorizes an exception.
4

Which statement about pain assessment should the nurse include when teaching a newly licensed nurse?

  • A. Most clients exaggerate pain.
  • B. Pain must have an identifiable source to justify opioids.
  • C. Objective data are essential to prove pain.
  • D. Pain is whatever the client says it is.
D Pain is subjective, and the client is the best source of pain information.
5

Which findings are adverse effects of opioid analgesics? Select all that apply.

  • A. Urinary incontinence.
  • B. Diarrhea.
  • C. Bradypnea.
  • D. Orthostatic hypotension.
  • E. Nausea.
C, D, E Opioids can cause respiratory depression, orthostatic hypotension, nausea/vomiting, sedation, urinary retention, and constipation.
A, B ATI highlights urinary retention and constipation, not urinary incontinence and diarrhea, as common opioid effects.
