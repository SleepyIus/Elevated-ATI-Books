Therapeutic Communication

Related Content

Purposeful, client-centered, goal-directed communication (not social or reciprocal). It travels through verbal (vocabulary, intonation, clarity, pacing) and nonverbal (posture, facial expression, eye contact, silence) channels — and the nonverbal can carry more impact.

Underlying Principles

  • Establish trust and a nonjudgmental environment
  • Observe for congruence between verbal and nonverbal messages
  • Empathy keeps the nurse supportive yet objective

Nursing Interventions

Techniques: active listening (SOLER), open-ended questions, silence, restating, reflecting, clarifying, focusing, summarizing, and offering general leads.

Avoid these blocks: giving advice, false reassurance, "why" questions, minimizing feelings, changing the subject, and value judgments.

Defense Mechanisms

Related Content

Unconscious strategies that manage anxiety; they are reversible and can be adaptive or maladaptive. Only altruism and sublimation are always healthy.

Examples: denial, repression, suppression, displacement, projection, reaction formation, rationalization, regression, compensation, undoing, identification, and introjection.

Underlying Principles

  • Adaptive vs maladaptive is judged by frequency, intensity, duration, and reality testing
  • Using a variety of mechanisms is healthier than the same reaction to every stressor
  • Maladaptive when they interfere with functioning, relationships, or reality

Nursing Interventions

  • Identify the mechanisms the client is using
  • Do not strip away defenses abruptly
  • Help the client develop healthier coping strategies
  • Promote insight gradually and support reality testing

Ethical Principles in MH Nursing

Related Content

Five bioethical principles guide practice: beneficence (do good), autonomy (right to decide), justice (fair treatment), fidelity (loyalty), and veracity (truthfulness) — with nonmaleficence (do no harm).

Underlying Principles

  • Apply the principles to every ethical dilemma
  • Balance autonomy against safety
  • HIPAA governs confidentiality
  • Tort law separates intentional torts (assault, battery, false imprisonment) from unintentional (negligence, malpractice)

Nursing Interventions

  • Identify the ethical conflict and the competing principles
  • Involve the interprofessional team or ethics committee
  • Maintain veracity and fidelity with the client
  • Protect autonomy while ensuring safety; document the reasoning

Client Rights and Commitment

Related Content

Mental health clients retain all civil rights plus protected treatment rights: informed consent, the right to refuse treatment, confidentiality, a written plan of care, communication with the outside, interpreter services, dignity, psychiatric advance directives, and care with the least restrictive interventions.

Admission ranges from voluntary to emergency and long-term involuntary commitment.

Underlying Principles

  • Care must be ethical, legal, and least restrictive
  • Involuntary commitment requires danger to self/others or grave disability, with due process
  • State laws vary and govern practice

Nursing Interventions

  • Verify consent and the client's competence
  • Honor refusal of treatment unless legal criteria override
  • Protect confidentiality — break only for duty to warn or mandated reporting
  • Advocate for least restrictive care; document thoroughly
  • Use seclusion/restraint only as a last resort with a provider order and frequent monitoring

Therapeutic Milieu

Related Content

The managed therapeutic environment that supports the nurse-client relationship — safe, structured, consistent, confidential, and reliable — designed to minimize stress and maximize therapeutic benefit.

Underlying Principles

  • Physical and emotional safety are foundational
  • Structure and predictability reduce anxiety
  • The entire environment is therapeutic, not just formal sessions

Nursing Interventions

  • Ensure physical safety — no sharps/substances, monitored visitors, prevent elopement
  • Set clear, consistent expectations and use planned de-escalation
  • Hold community meetings; promote client responsibility within limits

Therapeutic Nurse-Client Relationship

Related Content

A goal-directed, time-limited, client-focused relationship with clear boundaries, unfolding in three phases: orientation, working, and termination.

Underlying Principles

  • Differs fundamentally from social or intimate relationships
  • Transference: client relates to the nurse as a significant past figure
  • Countertransference: nurse projects past feelings onto the client
  • Empathy is therapeutic; sympathy is not

Nursing Interventions

  • Orientation: build trust, set the contract/boundaries/goals
  • Working: problem-solve, build coping, address resistance and transference
  • Termination: review progress, plan, process feelings and possible regression
  • Maintain professional boundaries and self-awareness throughout

Levels of Anxiety

Related Content

Anxiety progresses through four levels: mild (heightened perception and motivation), moderate (narrowed perception, learning still possible), severe (learning stops, focus on detail), and panic (loss of reality, disorganization).

Underlying Principles

  • As anxiety rises, the ability to learn and problem-solve falls
  • Nursing care is matched to the level of anxiety

Nursing Interventions

  • Mild/moderate: explore feelings and coping; open-ended questions
  • Severe/panic: stay with the client, low-stimulation environment, firm short directions, ensure safety
  • Defer health teaching until the acute anxiety subsides

Grief and Loss

Related Content

Grief is the response to loss. Types include anticipatory, normal/uncomplicated, complicated/maladaptive, and disenfranchised grief. Theories include Kübler-Ross's stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance), Bowlby, and Worden's tasks of mourning.

Underlying Principles

  • Grief is individual and nonlinear — stages need not occur in order
  • Culture and spirituality shape the experience
  • Complicated grief may require professional intervention

Nursing Interventions

  • Provide presence and active listening; encourage expression
  • Respect cultural and spiritual practices
  • Facilitate — never force — the grieving process
  • Support comfort and dignity for the dying client and family; identify complicated grief and refer

Crisis and Crisis Intervention

Related Content

A crisis occurs when usual coping fails; it is self-limiting (about 4–6 weeks). Types: maturational, situational, and adventitious.

Underlying Principles

  • Crisis intervention is short-term and present-focused
  • The goal is to return the client to the pre-crisis level of functioning
  • The nurse takes a more active, directive role than usual

Nursing Interventions

  • Assess safety first; identify the precipitating problem
  • Mobilize support systems and resources
  • Develop concrete, realistic coping steps

Stress and Coping

Related Content

Stress is the body's response to demand; eustress motivates while distress harms. Anxiety sits on a continuum: mild → moderate → severe → panic. The General Adaptation Syndrome moves through alarm, resistance, and exhaustion.

Underlying Principles

  • As anxiety rises, perception, learning, and problem-solving fall
  • Mild enhances perception; severe halts learning; panic loses touch with reality
  • Nursing care is matched to the anxiety level

Nursing Interventions

  • Mild/moderate: active listening, open-ended questions, explore coping
  • Severe/panic: stay with the client, low-stimulation environment, firm short statements, gross motor activity
  • Medications/restraints only after less restrictive measures fail
  • Teach relaxation, cognitive reframing, and assertiveness once calm

Diverse Practice Settings

Related Content

Mental health care spans a continuum from least to most restrictive — community/outpatient, partial hospitalization, residential, and acute inpatient. Settings include community mental health centers, psychiatric home care, ACT teams, crisis stabilization, and correctional facilities. Levels of prevention: primary (education, reduce incidence), secondary (early screening/treatment), tertiary (rehabilitation, relapse prevention).

Underlying Principles

  • Care occurs in the least restrictive setting that meets safety needs
  • Deinstitutionalization shifted care into the community
  • Continuity and case management coordinate services across the continuum

Nursing Interventions

  • Match the setting to acuity and safety needs
  • Coordinate care and discharge planning
  • Link clients to community resources; provide case management
  • Promote continuity across the continuum of care

Stress Management

Related Content

Stress is the body's response to demand: eustress (positive, motivating) versus distress (negative). The General Adaptation Syndrome moves through alarm → resistance → exhaustion. Coping may be problem-focused or emotion-focused.

Underlying Principles

  • Chronic distress harms physical and mental health
  • Building coping and resilience reduces impact
  • Relaxation lowers the physiologic stress response

Nursing Interventions

  • Teach relaxation — deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, meditation, mindfulness
  • Promote exercise, sleep, and time management
  • Teach assertiveness and cognitive reframing; encourage social support
  • Practice skills while the client is calm

Family and Community Violence

Related Content

Forms include child abuse/neglect, intimate partner violence, and elder abuse (physical, emotional, sexual, neglect, financial). The cycle of violence: tension-building → acute battering → honeymoon/reconciliation. Abuse occurs across all groups.

Underlying Principles

  • Safety is the priority
  • Nurses are mandatory reporters of suspected child and vulnerable-adult abuse
  • Care is trauma-informed and nonjudgmental; the survivor is never to blame

Nursing Interventions

  • Recognize indicators — inconsistent injury history, fear, a controlling partner, delayed care
  • Interview the client privately and safely; document objectively (direct quotes and findings)
  • Report suspected abuse per law; provide resources and safety planning
  • Respect a competent adult's autonomy in their decisions

Mental Status Examination (MSE)

Description of Procedure

The psychiatric equivalent of vital signs: a structured assessment of current mental functioning across appearance, behavior, speech, mood/affect, thought process, thought content, cognition, and insight/judgment.

Indications

  • Every psychiatric encounter to establish baseline and detect change
  • Screening for self-harm, harm to others, or risk of harm from others
  • Assessment of altered mood, thought process, cognition, perception, or judgment

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Provide privacy, reduce distractions, explain the purpose, and review safety concerns, current symptoms, medications, and baseline functioning when available.

Intra: Observe appearance/behavior/speech, ask about mood, assess affect, thought process/content, cognition, insight, and judgment, and ask directly about safety concerns when indicated.

Post: Document objective findings, compare with baseline, report safety concerns promptly, and update interventions based on risk and changes.

Interpretation of Findings

  • Findings guide diagnosis, care planning, communication, and safety measures.
  • Flat affect, disorganized thought, impaired judgment, perceptual disturbances, or suicidal/homicidal ideation require targeted follow-up.

Client Education

  • Explain that questions are routine and help the care team understand current functioning.
  • Encourage honest responses, especially about safety, mood, thoughts, and perceptions.

Potential Complications

  • Missed safety risk if screening is omitted or findings are documented vaguely
  • Escalation or delayed care if suicidal/homicidal ideation or psychosis is not acted on

Nursing Interventions

  • Use objective language and avoid judgmental labels.
  • Ask directly about suicidal and homicidal thoughts when safety is in question.
  • Compare current findings with baseline and prior documentation.
  • Notify provider/team immediately for acute safety concerns or major mental-status change.

Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)

Description of Procedure

A brief standardized cognitive screening tool that assesses orientation, registration, attention/calculation, recall, language, and visuospatial ability.

Indications

  • Screen for cognitive impairment such as dementia or delirium
  • Track cognition over time, especially in older adults
  • Clarify cognitive baseline when confusion or functional decline is suspected

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Provide a quiet setting, ensure glasses/hearing aids/interpreter needs are addressed, and explain that the tool screens cognition rather than intelligence.

Intra: Administer items consistently, score each section accurately, avoid coaching, and observe anxiety, fatigue, sensory limitations, and effort.

Post: Document score and context, compare with previous results, and communicate abnormal or declining scores for follow-up.

Interpretation of Findings

  • Lower scores suggest greater cognitive impairment, but results must be interpreted with education level, language, culture, sensory function, and clinical context.
  • A decline over time is more meaningful than one isolated score.

Client Education

  • Reassure the client that the screen is routine and not a pass/fail test.
  • Explain that family or caregivers may help compare current function with baseline.

Potential Complications

  • False low score from sensory deficits, language barriers, anxiety, pain, fatigue, or low literacy
  • Missed delirium if the score is treated as a diagnosis instead of a screening result

Nursing Interventions

  • Ensure sensory aids and communication supports are available before testing.
  • Use the same tool over time when trending cognition.
  • Document factors that may affect score validity.
  • Report acute cognitive change promptly because delirium can signal medical instability.

Standardized Rating Scales

Description of Procedure

Validated tools that quantify symptom severity and track trends, such as depression/anxiety scales, AIMS for abnormal involuntary movements, GDS for older-adult depression, and CIWA for alcohol withdrawal.

Indications

  • Establish baseline symptom severity
  • Monitor treatment response or adverse effects over time
  • Support objective documentation and clinical decision-making

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Select the correct tool for the client population and condition, explain the purpose, and create a setting that supports honest responses.

Intra: Administer consistently, score according to tool instructions, and combine the score with clinical observation and client report.

Post: Document score, date/time, tool used, relevant context, trend, and any provider notification or intervention.

Interpretation of Findings

  • Scores help guide treatment decisions and show trends.
  • Rising, falling, or threshold scores should be interpreted with clinical judgment and not used alone as a diagnosis.

Client Education

  • Explain how the tool helps track symptoms and treatment response.
  • Encourage honest answers rather than answers the client thinks staff want to hear.

Potential Complications

  • Wrong tool selection, over-reliance on score alone, inconsistent administration, or missed clinical change despite a stable score

Nursing Interventions

  • Use the same validated tool for trending when possible.
  • Pair scores with clinical observation and safety assessment.
  • Escalate concerning scores according to protocol.
  • Document score interpretation, interventions, and follow-up plan.

Substance Use Screening (CAGE)

Description of Procedure

A brief four-item screen for problematic alcohol use: Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, and Eye-opener.

Indications

  • Screening for possible alcohol use disorder
  • Assessment when substance use, withdrawal risk, injury, mood symptoms, or family concern is present
  • Baseline screening to guide further assessment and referral

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Provide privacy, use a nonjudgmental tone, explain that screening is routine, and assess immediate safety or withdrawal concerns.

Intra: Ask each CAGE question clearly, allow time for response, and pair results with broader alcohol/substance history.

Post: Document responses and score, notify provider or refer for further assessment when indicated, and monitor for withdrawal risk if use is significant.

Interpretation of Findings

  • Two or more affirmative answers suggest problem drinking and warrant further assessment.
  • CAGE is a screening tool, not a stand-alone diagnosis.

Client Education

  • Explain that honest answers guide care and are not meant to shame or punish.
  • Offer treatment resources, support options, and follow-up when screening is positive.

Potential Complications

  • Underreporting due to stigma or fear of consequences
  • Missed withdrawal risk or polysubstance use if screening is not followed by history and assessment

Nursing Interventions

  • Ask questions calmly and without judgment.
  • Assess for withdrawal, safety, co-occurring mental health needs, and readiness for change.
  • Provide referral/resources for positive screening.
  • Document exact responses, score, education, and follow-up actions.

Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS)

Description of Procedure

A standardized older-adult depression screening tool using simple yes/no responses and minimizing overlap with normal aging symptoms.

Indications

  • Depression screening for older adults
  • Baseline symptom severity and follow-up trend
  • Life changes, grief, social isolation, medical illness, or functional decline

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Provide privacy, ensure glasses/hearing aids are available, consider language/cognitive barriers, and explain that screening is routine.

Intra: Ask items clearly, allow time for responses, observe affect and engagement, and screen safety if depression indicators are present.

Post: Score and document results, trend over time, combine with MSE/suicide-risk screen/medical history, and notify provider for positive findings or safety concerns.

Interpretation of Findings

  • A higher positive screen supports further depression assessment.
  • GDS is a screening tool and should be interpreted with clinical context, grief, cognition, and medical conditions.

Client Education

  • Explain that depression is treatable and not a normal required part of aging.
  • Encourage honest answers and follow-up if sadness, hopelessness, isolation, or loss of interest is present.

Potential Complications

  • False results from sensory impairment, cognitive impairment, grief, illness, language barriers, or reluctance to disclose symptoms
  • Missed suicide risk if a positive depression screen is not followed by safety assessment

Nursing Interventions

  • Prepare the environment and communication supports before screening.
  • Combine GDS with MSE, safety assessment, medical review, and collateral information when appropriate.
  • Notify provider for positive screens or safety concerns.
  • Document score, trend, teaching, and follow-up plan.

Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS)

Description of Procedure

A structured observation tool used to screen for abnormal involuntary movements, including tardive dyskinesia, in clients receiving antipsychotic medications.

Indications

  • Baseline and ongoing monitoring for clients taking antipsychotics
  • New lip smacking, tongue movement, facial grimacing, trunk movement, or extremity movement
  • Medication review when extrapyramidal symptoms or tardive dyskinesia are suspected

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Review antipsychotic exposure, baseline movement symptoms, and timing of prior AIMS scores; explain the movement observation process.

Intra: Observe face, tongue, jaw, extremities, and trunk using the structured scale, and differentiate involuntary movement from anxiety, psychosis, or voluntary movement.

Post: Document scores and trend, notify provider for new/worsening movements, and reinforce not stopping medication abruptly without provider direction.

Interpretation of Findings

  • New or worsening involuntary movements require prompt medication review and follow-up monitoring.
  • Trends are more useful than isolated scores for detecting emerging tardive dyskinesia.

Client Education

  • Report abnormal movements early, including lip smacking, tongue movements, facial grimacing, or unusual limb/trunk movements.
  • Do not stop prescribed antipsychotics abruptly; medication changes require provider direction.

Potential Complications

  • Unrecognized tardive dyskinesia can become persistent.
  • Abrupt medication discontinuation can destabilize psychotic or mood symptoms.

Nursing Interventions

  • Complete baseline and periodic AIMS assessments for clients receiving antipsychotics.
  • Trend scores and describe observed movements objectively.
  • Notify provider for new or worsening movement symptoms.
  • Educate client/family about early reporting and medication safety.

Mental Health Across the Lifespan

Physical Development

Adolescence: puberty, rapid growth, increased sleep need. Adulthood: peak then gradual decline. Older adulthood: declining muscle/bone mass, slower metabolism, sensory changes — physical changes shape MH presentation and self-image.

Cognitive Development

Adolescence: Piaget's formal operations (abstract thinking emerges); identity exploration. Adulthood: sustained executive function. Older adulthood: processing speed declines but crystallized knowledge persists; the MMSE distinguishes normal aging from delirium or dementia.

Psychosocial Development

Erikson — MH-relevant stages:

  • Adolescence: identity vs role confusion — peer relationships, identity formation
  • Young adult: intimacy vs isolation
  • Middle adult: generativity vs stagnation
  • Older adult: integrity vs despair — life review, meaning-making

Age-Appropriate Activities

  • Adolescents: peer activities, supervised autonomy, creative/expressive outlets
  • Adults: meaningful work, intimate relationships, parenting, community engagement
  • Older adults: reminiscence, life review, intergenerational connection, structured cognitive stimulation

Immunizations

Per CDC schedule by age — supports overall wellness, which underpins mental health. Address vaccine-related anxiety with therapeutic communication.

Health Screening

  • Adolescents: HEADSSS, depression and anxiety screens (PHQ-9 modified for adolescents), suicide-risk screening with the C-SSRS
  • Adults: annual depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), substance use (CAGE / AUDIT)
  • Older adults: Geriatric Depression Scale, MMSE for cognition, social-isolation screen

Nutrition

  • Adolescents: screen for restrictive/binge patterns — eating-disorder risk peaks here
  • Adults: appetite changes can signal depression; alcohol/substance impact
  • Older adults: watch for weight loss, dehydration, and feeding difficulties (especially with dementia)

Injury Prevention

  • Adolescents: substance use, risk-taking, motor-vehicle safety, online safety; suicide-risk safety planning
  • Adults: workplace safety, substance use, intimate-partner-violence screening
  • Older adults: fall prevention, medication safety, home safety (especially with cognitive decline)

Children & Adolescent Mental Health

Physical Development

Rapid growth periods (infancy and adolescent puberty). Track developmental milestones; physical illness or disability impacts mental development. Sleep needs are highest in infancy and again in adolescence — chronic sleep deprivation is a major MH risk factor.

Cognitive Development

Piaget's stages:

  • Sensorimotor (birth–2): object permanence emerging
  • Preoperational (2–7): egocentric, magical thinking
  • Concrete operational (7–11): logical thinking about concrete events
  • Formal operational (12+): abstract reasoning, hypothetical thinking — also enables rumination, identity-questioning, cognitive distortions

Psychosocial Development

Erikson — pediatric stages:

  • Trust vs mistrust (infancy) — secure attachment
  • Autonomy vs shame (toddler)
  • Initiative vs guilt (preschool)
  • Industry vs inferiority (school age) — competence at tasks, peer comparison
  • Identity vs role confusion (adolescent) — peer belonging, identity exploration

Bullying, family disruption, abuse, and peer rejection are leading MH disruptors.

Age-Appropriate Activities

  • Infants/toddlers: attachment-promoting play, consistent caregivers, sensory exploration
  • Preschool: imaginative play, simple cooperative games, emotional vocabulary building
  • School age: structured cooperative activities, hobbies, mastery experiences
  • Adolescent: peer-group activities, supervised autonomy, creative/expressive outlets, sports

Immunizations

Per the CDC schedule. Address vaccine-related anxiety with developmentally-appropriate explanations; use brief, honest truth-telling. Avoid forced restraint when possible — use comfort positioning and distraction.

Health Screening

  • Toddlers: M-CHAT for autism at 18 and 24 months
  • School age: behavioral and learning screens; trauma exposure (ACEs) when indicated
  • Adolescents: PHQ-A for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety, CRAFFT for substance use, C-SSRS for suicide risk; HEADSSS psychosocial interview at well-visits

Nutrition

  • Infants: failure-to-thrive can signal attachment disruption
  • School age/adolescent: screen for restrictive, binge, or purging patterns — eating-disorder onset peaks in adolescence
  • Food insecurity is associated with elevated rates of depression and anxiety across all pediatric ages

Injury Prevention

  • Young children: car seats, helmets, choking hazards, drowning prevention, secure firearm/medication storage
  • Adolescents: distracted-driving, substance-use prevention, online safety, dating-violence screening
  • Suicide means restriction — especially firearms and medications — based on C-SSRS-informed safety planning

Older Adult Mental Health

Physical Development

Multiple chronic conditions are common; physical and mental health interact bidirectionally. Sensory loss (vision, hearing) increases isolation and depression risk. Sleep architecture changes with less deep sleep — but a true new pattern of insomnia or excessive sleeping often signals depression or dementia.

Cognitive Development

Normal aging: slower processing speed; mild benign forgetfulness; preserved crystallized knowledge, vocabulary, and reasoning.

Distinguish three patterns:

  • Normal aging — slow, stable, doesn't disrupt function
  • Delirium — acute, fluctuating, reversible cause
  • Dementia — gradual, progressive, persistent decline

Use MMSE or MoCA as cognitive screens.

Psychosocial Development

Erikson — older adulthood: integrity vs despair — life review, meaning-making, acceptance of one's life vs regret and bitterness.

Common stressors: retirement, widowhood, accumulated losses, loss of independence, fixed income, role changes from caregiver to recipient.

Age-Appropriate Activities

  • Reminiscence and life review — therapeutic for integrity work
  • Intergenerational connection — grandchildren, mentoring
  • Structured cognitive stimulation — puzzles, reading, conversation, music
  • Spiritual/religious community — strong protective factor
  • Gentle exercise — walking, tai chi, water aerobics, chair yoga

Immunizations

Influenza annually, pneumococcal (PCV/PPSV), shingles (zoster), Tdap booster every 10 years, COVID-19 boosters as recommended. Vaccine hesitancy in older adults sometimes signals depression, cognitive impairment, or MH stigma — explore with therapeutic communication.

Health Screening

  • Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) instead of PHQ-9 — fewer somatic items that overlap with normal aging
  • MMSE/MoCA for cognitive baseline and serial tracking
  • CAGE for late-onset alcohol misuse — often missed
  • C-SSRS — older men have the highest completed-suicide rate of any group
  • Elder-abuse screen — physical signs, financial irregularities, caregiver stress

Nutrition

  • Watch for unintended weight loss, dehydration, and dysphagia (especially with dementia)
  • Food insecurity in older adults is under-recognized
  • Appetite changes often signal depression or dementia progression
  • Alcohol use may rise after losses; affects nutrition and falls risk

Injury Prevention

  • Fall prevention — assess gait, vision, home environment (rugs, lighting, bathroom safety)
  • Medication safety — review against the Beers criteria; anticholinergics and benzodiazepines are high-risk
  • Driving safety — sensitive but essential to assess with cognitive decline
  • Suicide means restriction — especially firearms, given the high completed-suicide rate in older men

HEADSSS Adolescent Assessment

Physical Development

Puberty, rapid growth, changing body image, and increased sleep needs. Mental health assessment should include appetite, sleep, fatigue, and somatic complaints without assuming they are only developmental.

Cognitive Development

Abstract thinking and future orientation are emerging, but judgment and impulse control are still developing. Screening should assess risk-taking and decision patterns.

Psychosocial Development

Erikson's identity vs role confusion: peers, autonomy, values, sexuality, family expectations, and belonging strongly shape mental health.

Age-Appropriate Activities

  • School, work, sports, clubs, hobbies, and creative outlets
  • Peer connection with safe adult supervision and clear limits
  • Private time for confidential assessment with limits explained

Immunizations

Follow adolescent immunization schedule and use the visit to normalize mental health screening.

Health Screening

  • Screen mood, anxiety, substance use, eating patterns, self-injury, suicide risk, abuse, and safety
  • Explain confidentiality and its limits before asking sensitive questions

Nutrition

Assess restrictive eating, bingeing, purging, appetite change, food insecurity, and body-image concerns.

Injury Prevention

Assess motor-vehicle safety, substance use, violence exposure, unsafe relationships, online safety, and access to means of self-harm.

ADHD Developmental Considerations

Physical Development

High activity level, impulsive movement, and sleep disturbance can affect growth, safety, and family routines. Stimulant therapy requires growth, appetite, sleep, and cardiovascular monitoring.

Cognitive Development

ADHD interferes with sustained attention, organization, working memory, and completion of school tasks. Manifestations must be present before age 12 and occur in more than one setting.

Psychosocial Development

Impulsivity and inattention can impair peer relationships, school performance, self-esteem, and family interactions.

Age-Appropriate Activities

  • Structured play, sports, and supervised physical activity
  • Short tasks with breaks and immediate positive reinforcement
  • School routines that reduce distractions and support success

Immunizations

Routine childhood/adolescent immunizations; prepare the child with brief concrete explanations and predictable steps.

Health Screening

  • Gather input from parents and teachers
  • Assess comorbid anxiety, depression, learning issues, substance use, and sleep problems
  • Monitor height, weight, appetite, sleep, pulse, and blood pressure when stimulants are prescribed

Nutrition

Offer nutrient-dense meals when appetite is strongest; monitor weight and growth over time.

Injury Prevention

Address impulsive injury risk with helmets, traffic safety, medication storage, supervision, and clear home/school safety rules.

Maturational Crisis

Physical Development

Developmental transitions can alter sleep, appetite, energy, and stress tolerance. Examples include parenthood, midlife change, retirement, or loss of independence.

Cognitive Development

The client may have temporarily narrowed problem-solving and difficulty seeing options while usual coping is overwhelmed.

Psychosocial Development

A maturational crisis occurs during a normal developmental transition such as marriage, parenthood, retirement, or role change.

Age-Appropriate Activities

Activities should rebuild role confidence: parenting support, peer support, life review, community engagement, or skill-building based on the transition.

Immunizations

Maintain age-appropriate preventive care during transitions because stress can disrupt routine health maintenance.

Health Screening

  • Assess precipitating event, anxiety level, coping, supports, and safety
  • Ask directly about suicide or violence risk when indicated

Nutrition

Assess appetite, access to food, alcohol/substance use, and stress-related changes in intake.

Injury Prevention

Increase safety planning when crisis causes impulsivity, impaired judgment, substance use, family conflict, or self-harm risk.

SSRIs

Expected Pharmacological Action

Selectively block the reuptake of serotonin.

Therapeutic Use

First-line for depression and anxiety disorders; also OCD, PTSD, and panic disorder.

Complications

  • Sexual dysfunction, GI upset, weight change, hyponatremia
  • Serotonin syndrome
  • Early increase in suicidal ideation (black box)

Contraindications/Precautions

MAOIs (serotonin syndrome); caution with bleeding risk/NSAIDs; pregnancy considerations.

Interactions

MAOIs, other serotonergic agents, and NSAIDs/anticoagulants (bleeding risk).

Evaluation of Medication Effectiveness

Improved mood and anxiety over weeks.

Medication Administration

Morning dosing; taper to discontinue; takes 2–4+ weeks.

Nursing Interventions

  • Monitor for serotonin syndrome and suicidal ideation
  • Manage SSRI-induced bruxism

Client Education

Onset takes weeks; do not stop abruptly; report serotonin-syndrome signs or worsening mood.

SNRIs

Expected Pharmacological Action

Block reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine.

Therapeutic Use

Depression, anxiety, and neuropathic pain (duloxetine).

Complications

  • Nausea, insomnia, sexual dysfunction
  • May raise blood pressure; serotonin syndrome

Contraindications/Precautions

Uncontrolled hypertension; MAOIs; caution with hepatic impairment.

Interactions

MAOIs and other serotonergic agents (serotonin syndrome).

Evaluation of Medication Effectiveness

De-escalation Techniques

Description of Skill

Structured verbal, environmental, and nonverbal interventions used to reduce client agitation and prevent escalation to aggression — the least-restrictive response that precedes seclusion or restraint.

Indications

  • Client showing increased agitation, anger, or threatening behavior
  • Before any consideration of seclusion or restraint
  • Early signs of escalation (pacing, raised voice, clenched fists, intrusion on personal space)

Outcomes/Evaluation

Client returns toward baseline calm; aggression is averted; safety of client, staff, and other clients is preserved; less-restrictive measures successfully avert more restrictive interventions.

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Pre: Assess triggers, remove unnecessary stimulation or audience, and make sure staff have support and a clear exit route.
  • Intra: Use a calm voice, personal space, SOLER, active listening, realistic choices, and firm respectful limits.
  • Post: Debrief triggers and responses, document the behavior and interventions used, and update the prevention plan.

Client Education

Teach the client to identify early warning signs, ask for staff support before escalation, use agreed coping strategies, and understand that least-restrictive safety steps are used before seclusion or restraint.

Potential Complications

If de-escalation fails, more restrictive interventions (seclusion or restraint) may be needed. Nurse or client injury can occur if the technique is inadequate or the situation is misjudged. Recognize personal limits and request help early.

Nursing Interventions

  • Maintain a calm voice, slow movements, and open body language
  • Provide personal space (minimum 4–6 ft); never corner the client or block exits
  • Use SOLER and active listening; acknowledge feelings
  • Offer realistic choices to restore the client's sense of control
  • Set firm but respectful limits; avoid power struggles and argument
  • Recognize triggers and remove the client from the triggering environment when possible
  • Request additional staff support early; ensure a safe environment

Suicide Precautions

Description of Skill

Tiered observation and environmental-safety protocol applied to clients at risk for suicide, scaled by acuity. The level (close vs continuous 1:1) is driven by the C-SSRS or institutional risk stratification — not by intuition alone.

  • 1:1 (arm's length) — staff within arm's reach at all times, including bathroom and shower
  • Continuous/constant observation — within sight at all times
  • Close observation (q15) — visualized and documented every 15 minutes

Indications

  • Active suicidal ideation with plan and/or intent
  • Recent attempt or interrupted attempt
  • C-SSRS scoring above institutional threshold
  • Abrupt mood shifts (apparent calm) — may signal decisional clarity rather than improvement
  • Recent loss of a major protective factor (relationship, housing, identity)
  • Admission for suicidal crisis until risk is reassessed

Outcomes/Evaluation

Client remains safe from self-harm; risk level is reassessed regularly and documented; transition to a less-restrictive observation level occurs only with consistently documented risk reduction; client engages with safety planning and follow-up resources before discharge.

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Pre: Screen suicide risk, remove hazards and potential means, assign the ordered observation level, and communicate the level during handoff.
  • Intra: Maintain the required observation during bathroom use, showering, transport, and shift change; document observations with times and engage therapeutically.
  • Post: Reassess risk before reducing precautions, complete safety planning, and coordinate family/support teaching and follow-up before discharge.

Client Education

Explain that precautions are for safety, not punishment. Teach the client to tell staff when urges increase, use the safety plan, contact crisis supports such as 988, and involve trusted supports in means restriction when appropriate.

Potential Complications

  • Client may hide intent or feign improvement ("smile of impending death") to lower observation
  • Observation fatigue and lapses during shift change, transport, or bathroom use — these are high-risk moments
  • Loss of therapeutic rapport if the level is not explained as care, not punishment
  • No-suicide contracts have no evidence base and can substitute dangerously for real safety planning

Nursing Interventions

  • Means restriction — remove shoelaces, belts, sharps, glass, cords, hoarded medications; inspect personal items per protocol
  • Document observation explicitly with time stamps; never "chart by exception"
  • Use C-SSRS to communicate level across shifts; reassess at each shift and after any precipitating event
  • Provide therapeutic presence — engage, don't just watch
  • Coordinate transitions tightly (shift handoff, off-unit transport, procedures)
  • Engage the client in Stanley-Brown safety planning before discharge
  • Educate family on means restriction at home — especially firearms and medications

Therapeutic Use of Self & Active Listening

Description of Skill

Deliberate use of the nurse's personality, observation, and presence as the primary therapeutic instrument — the core skill of psychiatric nursing. Built on self-awareness, congruence, and active listening: conscious, full attention to verbal and nonverbal content without mentally rehearsing a response.

Indications

Every nurse-client psychiatric encounter, especially during the orientation and working phases of Peplau's nurse-client relationship model. Critical when building trust, eliciting suicidal ideation, processing trauma, supporting grief, or addressing resistance.

Outcomes/Evaluation

Client expresses feelings openly; demonstrates trust through disclosure; reports feeling heard and understood; engages more fully with the care plan; the therapeutic alliance deepens across sessions; the client internalizes new coping or insight.

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Pre: Use self-awareness to identify bias, countertransference risk, privacy needs, and enough time for a therapeutic exchange.
  • Intra: Use SOLER, silence, restating, reflecting, clarifying, summarizing, open-ended questions, and professional boundaries.
  • Post: Summarize key themes, document safety concerns and client responses, and seek supervision when boundaries or countertransference arise.

Client Education

Encourage honest sharing at the client's pace, explain that silence can be therapeutic, clarify professional boundaries, and invite the client to ask for clarification when communication feels unclear.

Potential Complications

  • Transference — client projects feelings about another person (parent, partner) onto the nurse
  • Countertransference — nurse responds based on the nurse's own history; can lead to overinvolvement or avoidance
  • Boundary blurring — inappropriate self-disclosure, gift-giving, extending contact outside the professional relationship
  • Blocking responses — false reassurance, advice-giving, "why" questions, premature problem-solving — all undermine trust
  • Burnout from emotional labor without supervision and self-care

Nursing Interventions

  • Use the SOLER framework: Sit squarely; Open posture; Lean in slightly; Eye contact (culturally adapted); Relax
  • Active-listening techniques: silence (most powerful), restating, reflecting feelings, clarifying, summarizing, focusing, exploring
  • Use open-ended questions ("What was that like for you?") rather than closed yes/no questions
  • Avoid blocking responses: false reassurance, advice, "why," agreement/disagreement, changing the subject
  • Limit self-disclosure: only when it serves the client, briefly, and immediately return focus to the client
  • Process transference, countertransference, and boundary tensions in clinical supervision
  • Maintain self-awareness through reflective practice and personal wellness

Crisis Safety Planning

Description of Skill

A collaboratively-developed, written, tiered plan that the client uses to navigate a suicidal crisis. The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention is the evidence-based standard, structured as six progressive steps from internal coping to external help. Replaces no-suicide contracts as the standard of care.

Indications

  • After any suicidal ideation, attempt, or hospitalization for suicide risk
  • Clients identified as moderate-to-high risk on the C-SSRS
  • Before discharge from psychiatric or emergency settings — non-negotiable
  • Proactively for clients with chronic suicidality
  • Following any precipitating event (loss, relationship rupture, anniversary)

Outcomes/Evaluation

Client carries the safety plan and references it during distress; reports use of internal coping or contacts during a precursor; family and supports are informed where appropriate; reduction in self-harm episodes; engagement with mental-health follow-up; the plan is revised after any precipitating event.

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Pre: Assess current risk, supports, access to lethal means, and readiness to collaborate; explain that the plan is used before crisis peaks.
  • Intra: Complete the six Stanley-Brown steps with concrete, realistic wording and rehearse how the client will use the plan.
  • Post: Provide an accessible copy, share with supports as allowed, and update the plan after triggers, discharge, or follow-up reassessment.

Client Education

Teach the client to use the safety plan early, keep it easy to access, call 988 or emergency services if the plan is not enough, and work with supports to remove or secure lethal means.

Potential Complications

  • Plan written but never internalized — exists on paper only
  • Means-restriction step skipped or refused — especially firearms; this step is the strongest evidence-based component
  • Contacts on the plan are unaware of their role or are not available when needed
  • The plan is not revised after a precipitating event
  • Provider relies on the safety plan as a substitute for ongoing assessment rather than a complement to it

Nursing Interventions

Walk the client through the six Stanley-Brown steps, collaboratively:

  1. Warning signs — personal triggers, thoughts, moods, situations that precede crisis
  2. Internal coping strategies — what the client can do alone to distract or self-soothe (walking, music, prayer, grounding, exercise)
  3. People and places that provide distraction — not for talking about suicide, just for connection
  4. People to ask for help — family or friends who know about the suicidality
  5. Professionals and agencies — therapist, prescriber, 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, local crisis services, emergency room
  6. Means restriction — collaboratively reduce access to lethal means (firearms, medications); even a brief time delay saves lives
  • Rehearse use of the plan; give a written copy; revise after any precipitating event
  • Share appropriately with family with the client's permission

Seclusion/Restraint Monitoring

Description of Skill

Safe, time-limited monitoring of a client in seclusion or restraint after less-restrictive interventions fail and immediate safety risk remains.

Indications

  • Immediate risk of harm to self, others, or the care environment
  • Less-restrictive interventions attempted and ineffective
  • Provider prescription and facility policy requirements are met

Outcomes/Evaluation

The client remains physically safe, regains behavioral control, and is released as soon as criteria are met.

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Pre: Attempt and document less-restrictive alternatives, verify the order and time limits, and assess baseline circulation, respirations, skin, pain, and mental status.
  • Intra: Monitor circulation, skin integrity, respirations, pain, vital signs, hydration, toileting, range of motion, and readiness for release per facility policy.
  • Post: Release as soon as criteria are met, debrief with the client, document required details, and revise the prevention plan.

Client Education

Explain the reason for seclusion or restraint, the specific behaviors needed for release, that rights and dignity remain protected, and how to ask for support before escalation in the future.

Potential Complications

Physical injury, impaired circulation, aspiration, psychological trauma, escalation, or legal/ethical violation if used without appropriate indication or monitoring.

Nursing Interventions

  • Use the least-restrictive option and the shortest duration possible
  • Monitor circulation, respirations, skin integrity, pain, vital signs, and mental status per policy
  • Offer food, fluids, toileting, and range of motion as allowed by safety and policy
  • Reassess frequently for readiness to release
  • Document the unsafe behavior, alternatives attempted, provider order, monitoring, medications, client response, and release time
  • Debrief after release and revise the prevention plan

Crisis Assessment and Action Planning

Description of Skill

A short-term, active nursing process that identifies the precipitating stressor, assesses safety, mobilizes support, and helps the client return toward pre-crisis functioning.

Indications

  • Usual coping has failed and the client is in acute disequilibrium
  • Maturational, situational, or adventitious crisis
  • Marked anxiety, impaired problem-solving, or safety concern

Outcomes/Evaluation

Immediate safety is maintained, anxiety decreases enough for problem-solving, supports are mobilized, and the client leaves with concrete short-term next steps.

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Pre: Identify immediate safety risk, the precipitating event, current coping, supports, developmental needs, and cultural context.
  • Intra: Use a calm presence, simple questions, active listening, clear limits, and one or two realistic short-term actions.
  • Post: Mobilize supports and resources, confirm follow-up, document risk and the action plan, and reassess safety.

Client Education

Teach that crisis is time-limited, review personal warning signs and coping steps, identify support contacts, and explain when to use crisis services, 988, the emergency department, or other urgent help.

Potential Complications

Escalation to self-harm, violence, severe anxiety, substance use, impaired judgment, or inability to access support if safety is not assessed early.

Nursing Interventions

  • Assess safety first, including suicide or homicide risk when indicated
  • Use a calm presence, simple questions, active listening, and clear limits
  • Identify the precipitating event, current coping, supports, and immediate needs
  • Help the client choose one or two realistic actions for the next hours or days
  • Mobilize family, community, crisis, or interprofessional resources
  • Document risk assessment, plan, supports contacted, and follow-up instructions

Anxiety Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Excessive fear/worry that impairs function — GAD, panic disorder, phobias, OCD, and body dysmorphic disorder.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of GABA, serotonin, and norepinephrine with a heightened amygdala-driven fear response.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Stress management and sleep hygiene
  • Limit caffeine and stimulants
  • Early screening and intervention

Risk Factors

  • Family history; female sex
  • Chronic stress or trauma
  • Substance use; other anxiety/personality disorders

Expected Findings

  • GAD: excessive worry ≥ 6 months, restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance
  • Panic attack: palpitations, dyspnea, chest discomfort, fear of dying

Laboratory Tests

No specific test; rule out medical causes (thyroid, cardiac) as indicated.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and standardized anxiety rating scales.

Safety Considerations

Assess self-harm risk (especially in body dysmorphic disorder); ensure safety during panic.

Complications

Functional impairment, depression, substance use, and social isolation.

Nursing Care

  • During panic — stay with the client, remain calm and quiet, minimize stimuli
  • Use open-ended communication; avoid "why" questions and advice
  • Defer health teaching until the acute anxiety subsides

Medications

SSRIs/SNRIs (first-line, long-term), buspirone (GAD), benzodiazepines (short-term), hydroxyzine, and beta-blockers (situational).

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT, exposure/systematic desensitization, and relaxation training.

Client Education

  • Medications take weeks; do not stop abruptly
  • Practice relaxation and coping; limit caffeine

Interprofessional Care

Therapy, psychiatry, and support groups; primary care for medical rule-out.

Serotonin dysregulation with involvement of cortico-striatal-thalamic circuits.

  • Early recognition
  • Stress reduction

  • Family history
  • Onset often in childhood/early adulthood
  • Association with tics

  • Time-consuming rituals (checking, washing, ordering, counting)

Laboratory Tests

None specific.

Clinical interview; Y-BOCS severity scale.

Safety Considerations

Complications

Depression and marked impairment in daily functioning.

Nursing Care

  • Allow time for rituals initially; do not abruptly stop them
  • Set gradual limits; reduce anxiety to decrease compulsions
  • Provide structure and routine

SSRIs (often higher doses) and clomipramine.

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT with exposure and response prevention (ERP).

Client Education

Adherence to therapy; medications take several weeks; understand the rationale for ERP.

Interprofessional Care

Therapy plus psychiatry; family education.

None specific.

Major Depressive Disorder

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

At least five findings (depressed mood, anhedonia, sleep/appetite change, fatigue, worthlessness, poor concentration) for ≥ 2 weeks.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, with neuroendocrine and genetic contributions.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Screening (e.g., PHQ-9)
  • Exercise, social connection, stress management
  • Treat comorbid medical illness

Risk Factors

  • Female sex; family history
  • Chronic medical illness; recent loss
  • Prior depression; substance use

Expected Findings

  • Depressed mood, anhedonia, psychomotor changes
  • Fatigue, sleep/appetite disturbance
  • Worthlessness/guilt, poor concentration, hopelessness

Laboratory Tests

No specific test; rule out medical causes (thyroid, anemia, vitamin deficiency).

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and depression rating scales (PHQ-9, Geriatric Depression Scale).

Safety Considerations

Safety is the priority. Assess suicide risk directly; risk is greatest early in treatment and as energy returns before mood lifts. Use one-to-one observation when indicated and remove potentially harmful items from the environment.

Complications

Suicide, functional impairment, substance use, and a chronic or recurrent course.

Nursing Care

  • Ensure safety; use simple, direct communication and allow time to respond
  • Promote ADLs, nutrition, and sleep; provide structure
  • Set small, achievable goals; spend time even in silence

Medications

SSRIs (first-line), SNRIs, atypicals (bupropion, mirtazapine), TCAs, MAOIs. Monitor for emerging suicidal ideation (black box); effect takes weeks.

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT, interpersonal therapy, ECT for severe/refractory cases, and light therapy (seasonal pattern).

Client Education

  • Medications take 2–4+ weeks; do not stop abruptly

  • Understand the acute, continuation, and maintenance phases

Therapy, psychiatry, social work, support groups, and crisis resources.

Bipolar Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Mood disorder cycling between mania/hypomania and depression: bipolar I (full mania), bipolar II (hypomania + depression), and cyclothymia.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin, with genetic and neurobiological contributions.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Sleep regulation and stress management
  • Medication adherence
  • Avoid stimulants and caffeine

Risk Factors

  • Family history
  • Onset in early adulthood
  • Substance use

Expected Findings

  • Mania: elevated/irritable mood, grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, risky behavior, possible psychosis
  • Depression: findings as in MDD

Laboratory Tests

Lithium level monitoring; renal and thyroid function; valproate levels and LFTs.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and mood charting; rule out medical/substance causes.

Safety Considerations

Monitor for escalating behavior (priority), exhaustion, and dehydration; assess suicide risk in the depressive phase; provide a low-stimulation environment.

Complications

Injury, exhaustion, suicide, and financial/legal consequences of risky behavior.

Nursing Care

  • Set consistent, firm, concise limits; redirect energy
  • Low-stimulation environment; protect from exhaustion
  • High-calorie finger foods; decline gifts matter-of-factly

Medications

Lithium (first-line, narrow range), anticonvulsants (valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine), and atypical antipsychotics.

Therapeutic Procedures

Psychotherapy, psychoeducation, and ECT for severe or refractory mania.

Client Education

  • Take medications consistently — not only when symptomatic
  • Sleep changes can signal relapse; anhedonia signals depressive relapse
  • Lithium: maintain fluid/sodium intake and get regular monitoring; avoid caffeine

Interprofessional Care

Psychiatry, therapy, social work, and support groups.

Schizophrenia / Psychotic Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Loss of contact with reality with positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech/behavior) and negative symptoms (flat affect, avolition, anhedonia, alogia, withdrawal).

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dopamine dysregulation (mesolimbic excess), with genetic, neurodevelopmental, and environmental contributions.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Early intervention and medication adherence
  • Stress reduction and family education

Risk Factors

  • Family history; onset late teens–early 20s
  • Prenatal/birth complications; cannabis use

Expected Findings

  • Hallucinations (often auditory) and delusions
  • Disorganized thought/speech (loose/clang associations, neologisms)
  • Negative symptoms; impaired insight (anosognosia)

Laboratory Tests

None diagnostic; baseline metabolic panel before antipsychotics; clozapine requires ANC monitoring.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview; rule out substance-induced or medical psychosis.

Safety Considerations

Assess for command hallucinations to harm self or others → one-to-one observation first; provide a calm, low-stimulation environment.

Complications

Self-harm/harm to others, medication nonadherence, relapse, social/occupational decline, and metabolic effects.

Nursing Care

  • Ask directly about hallucinations and command content; focus on feelings
  • Do not argue about the reality of delusions
  • Consistent staff, build trust, reality orientation, promote adherence

Medications

Antipsychotics — typical (positive symptoms, EPS) and atypical (positive + negative, metabolic effects); long-acting injectables for adherence; clozapine for resistant cases (ANC monitoring).

Therapeutic Procedures

Social skills training, supportive therapy, psychoeducation, and Assertive Community Treatment (ACT).

Client Education

  • Long-term adherence — do not stop when feeling better
  • Report EPS and other side effects; attend metabolic monitoring
  • Recognize relapse signs

Interprofessional Care

Psychiatry, case management, ACT, family support, and vocational rehabilitation.

Genetic, neurobiological, and developmental/environmental factors (e.g., early trauma in borderline PD).

Laboratory Tests

None specific.

Clinical interview and assessment of longitudinal patterns.

Self-harm, substance use, relationship/occupational dysfunction, and comorbid depression/anxiety.

Neurocognitive Disorders (Delirium & Dementia)

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Disrupted thinking, memory, and processing. Delirium is acute, fluctuating, and reversible; dementia is gradual, progressive, and irreversible.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Delirium: acute disturbance (infection, metabolic, drugs). Dementia (Alzheimer's): neuronal degeneration with amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Manage cardiovascular risk; cognitive and social engagement
  • Prevent/treat delirium causes; review medications

Risk Factors

  • Delirium: older age, infection, polypharmacy, surgery, dehydration
  • Dementia: age, family history, vascular disease

Expected Findings

  • Delirium: acute altered LOC, hallucinations, restlessness, fluctuating course
  • Dementia: gradual memory loss, the 4 A's (amnesia, aphasia, apraxia, agnosia), sundowning

Laboratory Tests

Delirium: CBC, electrolytes, urinalysis (UTI), drug levels to find the cause. Dementia: rule out reversible causes (B12, thyroid).

Diagnostic Procedures

MMSE/cognitive testing; neuroimaging for dementia; identify the delirium cause.

Safety Considerations

Home safety — locks high on exit doors, mattress on the floor, lighting over stairs, remove throw rugs, lock cleaning supplies; wandering precautions (ID bracelet, alarms); fall prevention.

Complications

Injury, falls, aspiration, infection, and caregiver burnout; untreated delirium leads to functional decline.

Nursing Care

  • Delirium: identify and treat the cause, ensure safety, reorient
  • Dementia: introduce yourself, use simple positive statements, redirect (don't confront or ask "why")
  • Maintain routine and manage sundowning

Medications

Dementia: donepezil/cholinesterase inhibitors (bedtime, avoid NSAIDs) and memantine (moderate–severe); avoid anticholinergics; cautious antipsychotic use for severe agitation.

Therapeutic Procedures

Cognitive stimulation, reminiscence, structured routine, and caregiver support/respite.

Client Education

  • Caregiver education and respite resources; home safety
  • Medication teaching; delirium is reversible if the cause is treated

Interprofessional Care

Geriatrics, neurology, social work, home health, and caregiver support resources.

Substance Use and Addictive Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Repeated substance use causing impairment over a 12-month period — loss of control, continued use despite problems, tolerance, withdrawal, and relapse.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of the dopaminergic reward pathway with neuroadaptation leading to tolerance and dependence.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Screening (CAGE) and education
  • Early intervention and relapse prevention

Risk Factors

  • Family history, mental illness, trauma
  • Peer/environmental influence; early use
  • Older adults after life-change stressors

Expected Findings

  • Alcohol withdrawal: tremors, vomiting, restlessness, tachycardia, hypertension
  • Opioid withdrawal: flu-like symptoms
  • Stimulant intoxication: agitation, tachycardia, dilated pupils

Laboratory Tests

Blood alcohol level, urine drug screen, LFTs, electrolytes; assess thiamine status in alcohol use.

Diagnostic Procedures

Screening tools (CAGE), clinical interview, and withdrawal assessment (CIWA).

Safety Considerations

Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal → seizure precautions (priority); delirium tremens at 48–72 hr is an emergency; give thiamine before glucose to prevent Wernicke; monitor vital signs.

Complications

DTs/seizures, Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, overdose, relapse, and organ damage.

Nursing Care

  • Manage safe withdrawal; use a nonjudgmental approach
  • Assess for codependency; promote abstinence
  • Support recovery and family involvement

Medications

Withdrawal: benzodiazepines (alcohol), carbamazepine (seizures), clonidine (opioid/autonomic). Abstinence: disulfiram, naltrexone, acamprosate. Opioid: methadone, buprenorphine, naloxone. Tobacco: NRT, varenicline, bupropion.

Therapeutic Procedures

12-step programs (AA), Al-Anon (family), CBT, motivational interviewing, and group therapy.

Client Education

  • Abstinence is the goal; address codependency
  • Disulfiram — avoid all alcohol-containing products
  • Relapse prevention and family involvement

Interprofessional Care

Addiction specialist, psychiatry, social work, and recovery programs.

Eating Disorders

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Multifactorial — neurobiological, genetic, psychological, and sociocultural, with serotonin involvement.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Promote a healthy body image
  • Early screening; address cognitive distortions

Risk Factors

  • Female sex; adolescence/young adulthood
  • Family history; perfectionism
  • Trauma; sociocultural pressures

Expected Findings

  • Anorexia: restriction, amenorrhea, lanugo, dry skin, bradycardia, cold intolerance
  • Bulimia: binge-purge cycles, near-normal weight, dental erosion, parotid swelling, Russell's sign

Laboratory Tests

Electrolytes (hypokalemia from purging) and cardiac monitoring; assess for metabolic disturbance.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview, ECG (dysrhythmia risk), and electrolyte panel.

Cardiac dysrhythmias, electrolyte imbalance, refeeding syndrome, and suicide.

Medications

SSRIs (especially for bulimia); treat comorbid depression/anxiety.

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT, family-based therapy, and team-managed nutritional rehabilitation.

Client Education

  • Focus on health and coping; address cognitive distortions
  • Family involvement supports recovery

Interprofessional Care

Dietitian, psychiatry, therapy, and ongoing medical monitoring.

Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Physical symptoms or health preoccupations not fully explained by a medical condition — somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety, conversion, and factitious disorders.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Psychological stress expressed through physical manifestations; not consciously produced (except in factitious disorder and malingering).

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Stress management and healthy coping
  • Address underlying anxiety or trauma

Risk Factors

  • Age 16–25
  • Anxiety disorders
  • Childhood trauma or abuse

Expected Findings

  • Somatic symptom disorder: distressing physical symptoms plus excessive thoughts/anxiety
  • Illness anxiety: preoccupation with having a serious illness
  • Conversion: neurological symptoms after a stressor (la belle indifference)
  • Factitious: falsified illness for the sick role

Laboratory Tests

Workup is negative or disproportionate to symptoms; avoid unnecessary repeat testing.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview; rule out medical causes appropriately while avoiding over-testing.

Safety Considerations

Monitor safety as indicated; factitious disorder imposed on another is abuse and must be reported; assess for depression/self-harm.

Complications

Functional impairment, unnecessary procedures, depression, and (in factitious-by-proxy) harm to a dependent.

Nursing Care

  • Teach alternative coping; set reasonable time limits on symptom discussion
  • Encourage communication; do not confront symptoms as "not real"
  • Avoid reinforcing secondary gain

Medications

SSRIs for comorbid anxiety or depression.

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT, regular scheduled visits with a single provider, and stress management.

Client Education

  • Coping strategies and the role of stress
  • Consistent, coordinated care

Interprofessional Care

Primary care plus psychiatry, with a consistent single provider to limit fragmentation.

Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

Description of Procedure

Electrical current induces a brief generalized seizure under anesthesia. A course is typically 2-3 times/week for 6-12 treatments; it reduces relapse but is not a cure.

Indications

  • Major depression unresponsive to medication
  • Bipolar disorder, including rapid cycling and severe mania
  • Acute suicidality or life-threatening refusal to eat/drink when rapid response is needed

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Verify informed consent, NPO status, baseline assessment, dentures/jewelry removal, voiding, and anesthesia readiness.

Intra: Assist with short-acting anesthetic, muscle relaxant, airway support, seizure monitoring, and safety precautions during treatment.

Post: Monitor airway, vital signs, LOC, orientation, memory effects, fall risk, and safe ambulation after recovery.

Outcomes/Evaluation

  • Mood and functional status improve over the treatment course.
  • Suicidal intensity or severe depressive/mania symptoms decrease.
  • Client recovers from anesthesia safely after each treatment.

Client Education

  • Explain the treatment schedule and that benefit is monitored across a course, not usually after one session.
  • Teach that transient confusion and short-term memory problems can occur.
  • Reinforce that consent can be withdrawn according to policy and law.

Potential Complications

  • Transient short-term memory loss, confusion, headache, muscle soreness, nausea, or anesthesia-related risk
  • Falls or injury if post-anesthesia confusion is not managed
  • Rare cardiovascular or airway complications

Nursing Interventions

  • Maintain NPO/anesthesia precautions and verify consent before treatment.
  • Reorient frequently after treatment and maintain fall precautions until fully awake and steady.
  • Compare mood, cognition, safety risk, and adverse effects with baseline.
  • Document seizure/treatment response, recovery status, teaching, and provider notification if complications occur.

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)

Description of Procedure

Noninvasive magnetic stimulation of cortical neurons. No anesthesia is required; sessions are usually about 30-40 min and are repeated several times per week for several weeks.

Indications

  • Major depression, especially when medication response is insufficient or adverse effects limit options

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Screen for seizure history and implanted metal or magnetic-sensitive devices; remove metal items and explain that the client remains awake.

Intra: Position the client, monitor comfort, headache/scalp discomfort, and anxiety, and support completion of the session.

Post: Assess headache, scalp discomfort, mood response over time, and readiness to resume normal activities.

Outcomes/Evaluation

  • Mood improves over the treatment course.
  • Client tolerates sessions without significant adverse effects.
  • Client returns to normal activity after treatment.

Client Education

  • Teach that TMS is noninvasive and does not require anesthesia or NPO status.
  • Explain that repeated sessions over several weeks are usually needed.
  • Report severe headache, seizure-like activity, or unexpected neurologic symptoms.

Potential Complications

  • Scalp discomfort, headache, facial muscle twitching, anxiety, or rare seizure

Nursing Interventions

  • Screen for contraindications before treatment.
  • Remove magnetic-sensitive items and monitor client comfort during the session.
  • Track mood response across visits rather than judging one isolated treatment.
  • Report severe or unusual neurologic symptoms immediately.

Family Therapy

Crisis Intervention

Description of Procedure

A short-term, structured, present-focused intervention that helps a person in crisis regain safety, reduce anxiety, mobilize support, and return toward baseline functioning.

Indications

  • Acute crisis with overwhelmed coping
  • Situational, maturational, or adventitious crisis
  • Marked anxiety, impaired problem-solving, or safety concern

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Assess immediate safety first, including suicide/homicide risk when indicated; establish rapport and identify the precipitating event.

Intra: Explore feelings and coping, mobilize support systems/resources, and develop concrete realistic steps using an active/directive style as needed.

Post: Confirm short-term plan, supports, follow-up referrals, and reassess safety before ending the encounter.

Outcomes/Evaluation

  • Anxiety decreases enough for problem-solving.
  • Immediate safety is maintained.
  • Client identifies one or two concrete next steps and support resources.

Client Education

  • Teach that crisis is time-limited and help is available.
  • Review coping skills, warning signs, support contacts, and crisis resources.
  • Encourage follow-up even after distress decreases.

Potential Complications

  • Escalation, self-harm, harm to others, substance use, severe anxiety, or inability to access support

Nursing Interventions

  • Prioritize safety assessment before problem solving.
  • Use calm, simple, concrete communication.
  • Mobilize family, community, crisis, or interprofessional resources.
  • Document risk assessment, action plan, supports contacted, and follow-up instructions.

Seclusion and Restraint

Description of Procedure

Restrictive interventions used only as a last resort to protect a client or others from imminent harm. Seclusion, physical restraint, and chemical restraint must follow least-restrictive principles and facility/legal requirements.

Indications

  • Imminent danger to self or others
  • Less-restrictive interventions attempted and ineffective
  • Provider order and facility policy requirements are met

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Attempt de-escalation and less-restrictive alternatives first; verify time-limited order requirements and baseline physical/mental status.

Intra: Explain the reason, maintain dignity, assign one communicator, and monitor circulation, vital signs, hydration, toileting, range of motion, and readiness for release.

Post: Release at the earliest safe time, debrief with client/staff, document thoroughly, and revise prevention plan.

Outcomes/Evaluation

  • Client and others remain safe.
  • Restrictive intervention is discontinued as soon as imminent risk resolves.
  • Required monitoring and time-limited order standards are met.

Client Education

  • Explain the reason for use and criteria for release.
  • Reinforce that rights and dignity remain protected.
  • Debrief afterward and identify alternatives for future escalation.

Potential Complications

  • Physical injury, circulatory compromise, aspiration, psychological trauma, escalation, or death if improperly applied

Nursing Interventions

  • Use only after less-restrictive interventions fail.
  • Monitor physical status, safety, and readiness for release per policy.
  • Offer hydration, toileting, ROM, and dignity-preserving care.
  • Document alternatives attempted, order, monitoring, response, release, and debriefing.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy & Exposure

Description of Procedure

Cognitive behavioral therapy helps clients identify and change distorted thinking and maladaptive behavior. Exposure-based methods include graded exposure/systematic desensitization and flooding when clinically appropriate.

Indications

Suicide Risk Assessment & Prevention

Description of Procedure

A structured suicide risk assessment paired with protective interventions. Validated tools such as the C-SSRS standardize questions about ideation, intensity, behavior, and lethality. Asking directly about suicide does not increase risk.

Indications

  • Depression, hopelessness, prior attempt, or suicidal statements/behaviors
  • Routine screening in primary care, emergency, inpatient, and psychiatric settings
  • Any change suggesting increased risk, including sudden calm after agitation or worsening substance use

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Ensure privacy and immediate safety; use a validated tool such as C-SSRS to ask directly about ideation, plan, intent, prior behavior, risk factors, and protective factors.

Intra: Document the client's own words, determine observation level, remove means, maintain supportive presence, and collaborate on a safety plan.

Post: Reassess risk, communicate handoff clearly, involve supports as appropriate, and arrange follow-up/crisis resources.

Outcomes/Evaluation

  • Risk level is identified and matched to safety interventions.
  • Client remains safe from self-harm.
  • Safety plan, observation level, means restriction, and follow-up are documented.

Client Education

  • Encourage direct communication about suicidal thoughts and urges.
  • Teach crisis resources, including 988 or emergency services if risk escalates.
  • Involve trusted supports and explain means restriction when appropriate.

Potential Complications

  • Self-harm or suicide attempt if risk is underestimated, observation lapses, or the environment is not secured

Nursing Interventions

  • Ask directly and calmly about suicide risk.
  • Never dismiss suicidal statements; document exact words.
  • Remove potential means and apply the ordered observation level.
  • Complete safety planning, handoff communication, and follow-up referrals.

Sexual Assault & Survivor Care

Description of Procedure

Coordinated, survivor-centered care after sexual assault, including emotional support, safety, medical assessment, optional forensic evidence collection by a trained examiner, prophylaxis, and connection to resources. The survivor is never to blame.

Indications

  • A client disclosing recent or past sexual assault
  • Need for safety assessment, medical care, STI/pregnancy prophylaxis, advocacy, or optional forensic evidence collection

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

Pre: Ensure immediate safety, privacy, trauma-informed communication, informed consent, and access to SANE/advocacy resources.

Intra: Respect the survivor's choices and pace; preserve evidence per protocol only with consent; address injuries, pregnancy risk, STI prophylaxis, and emotional needs.

Post: Arrange follow-up, counseling/advocacy resources, safety planning, and ongoing assessment for PTSD, depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation.

Outcomes/Evaluation

  • Survivor receives nonjudgmental, trauma-informed care and retains control over decisions.
  • Medical needs and optional forensic options are addressed.
  • Follow-up resources and safety plan are in place.

Client Education

  • Reinforce that the assault was not the survivor's fault.
  • Explain options without pressure, including forensic exam, reporting, prophylaxis, counseling, and advocacy.
  • Teach that varied emotional responses are normal and support remains available.

Potential Complications

  • PTSD, depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, retraumatization, injury, STI, pregnancy, or loss of trust if care is coercive

Nursing Interventions

  • Use calm, nonjudgmental, trauma-informed communication.
  • Obtain consent before each assessment, treatment, evidence collection, or reporting step.
  • Preserve evidence only according to protocol and survivor choice.
  • Document objectively and arrange medical, advocacy, counseling, and safety follow-up.

Mental Health

Defining Characteristics

Emotional, cognitive, social, and behavioral functioning that supports adaptation, fulfilling relationships, productive work, and contribution to community. Includes resilience, effective coping, sense of well-being, and the ability to handle ordinary stress.

Antecedents

  • Biological: neurotransmitter balance, genetic predisposition, neural integrity
  • Psychological: self-esteem, coping skills, secure attachment, sense of mastery
  • Sociocultural: supportive relationships, community connection, economic security, cultural identity

Negative Consequences

When impaired: emotional distress, functional impairment, disruption of relationships and work, risk for self-harm and suicide, comorbid medical illness, and social isolation.

Related Concepts

Mental illness, recovery, wellness, resilience, coping, stigma, well-being, and self-actualization.

Exemplars

  • Positive: a person with secure social connections, effective stress management, a sense of purpose, and the ability to seek help when needed
  • Negative (illness exemplar): severe depressive symptoms that impair daily life, or anxiety that prevents normal function

Coping

Defining Characteristics

The cognitive and behavioral effort to manage demands that tax or exceed personal resources (Lazarus & Folkman). Operates in two complementary modes:

  • Problem-focused — directed at changing the stressor itself (planning, action, seeking information)
  • Emotion-focused — directed at managing the emotional response (relaxation, reframing, social support)

Coping is adaptive when matched to the controllability of the stressor: problem-focused for changeable stressors, emotion-focused for uncontrollable ones (e.g., terminal illness, grief).

Antecedents

  • Biological: temperament, autonomic reactivity, baseline neurotransmitter regulation
  • Psychological: cognitive appraisal of the stressor (threat vs challenge), self-efficacy, prior coping success
  • Social: availability of support, modeling from family and peers
  • Cultural: coping norms (collective vs individual), spiritual/religious frameworks

Negative Consequences

When coping is overwhelmed or maladaptive:

  • Maladaptive coping — substance use, avoidance, denial, social withdrawal, immature defense mechanisms (projection, splitting, acting out)
  • Chronic distress with downstream anxiety, depression, PTSD
  • Somatic symptoms (insomnia, fatigue, GI distress, headache)
  • Crisis when usual coping mechanisms fail — the precise definition of a psychiatric crisis

Related Concepts

Stress, adaptation, defense mechanisms (mature vs immature), resilience, crisis, anxiety, grief, post-traumatic growth.

Exemplars

  • Positive (adaptive): a nursing student facing exam stress uses problem-focused coping (study schedule, tutoring, study group) AND emotion-focused coping (sleep hygiene, exercise, support from peers)
  • Positive (uncontrollable stressor): a client with a terminal diagnosis uses spiritual practice, life review, and family connection (emotion-focused) when problem-focused approaches no longer apply
  • Negative (maladaptive): a client uses alcohol to numb anxiety, avoiding the triggering situations until they accumulate into crisis
  • Negative (immature defense): a client uses projection — blaming others for their own distress — preventing the development of insight

Therapeutic Relationship

Defining Characteristics

A goal-directed, time-limited, professional relationship focused on client growth and wellness. Built on trust, empathy, genuineness, respect, and confidentiality. Distinguished from social, intimate, or familial relationships by its asymmetry — the focus is always on the client.

Peplau's phases:

  • Orientation — establishing trust, contract, expectations
  • Working — identifying problems, exploring feelings, building insight, learning new behaviors
  • Termination — planned closure, addressing feelings about ending, consolidating gains

Antecedents

  • Nurse's self-awareness and therapeutic use of self
  • Client's readiness for engagement
  • Appropriate setting, privacy, and time
  • Clear role definition and explicit boundaries
  • Cultural responsiveness and humility
  • Confidentiality framework understood by both

Negative Consequences

When the relationship is impaired or violated:

  • Loss of trust; ruptured alliance; treatment dropout
  • Unaddressed transference and countertransference distorting care
  • Boundary violations — emotional (over-self-disclosure), physical, financial, or sexual
  • Nurse burnout from emotional labor without supervision
  • Client harm: dependency, exploitation, or re-traumatization

Related Concepts

Therapeutic communication, therapeutic use of self, professional boundaries, transference, countertransference, milieu therapy, nurse-client phases (Peplau), empathy, trust.

Exemplars

  • Positive (orientation phase): nurse introduces self, role, purpose, time-frame, and confidentiality limits; collaboratively sets goals with the client
  • Positive (working phase): nurse maintains consistent presence, processes a client's grief using active listening — without rescuing, judging, or premature reassurance
  • Positive (termination): termination is named early, processed, and gains are consolidated; closure happens with notice, not abruptly
  • Negative (boundary): nurse befriends or messages a client on social media after discharge — a boundary violation regardless of intent
  • Negative (countertransference): nurse over-self-discloses about own mental-health struggles, shifting the focus from client to nurse

Community Prevention Levels

Defining Characteristics

A community mental health framework that matches interventions to prevention level: primary prevents problems, secondary detects and treats early, and tertiary reduces disability and relapse after diagnosis.

Antecedents

  • Community assessment and identification of risk groups
  • Availability of screening, crisis, outpatient, rehabilitation, and case-management resources
  • Clear understanding of whether the client is at risk, newly symptomatic, or living with an established disorder

Negative Consequences

If the prevention level is mismatched, clients can miss education, screening, early treatment, relapse prevention, or rehabilitation supports.

Related Concepts

Community mental health, case management, continuity of care, least-restrictive setting, early detection, recovery, and rehabilitation.

Exemplars

  • Primary: stress-management education or mental health promotion in a school or community setting
  • Secondary: depression, suicide-risk, or substance-use screening with prompt referral
  • Tertiary: psychosocial rehabilitation, relapse-prevention planning, or support for a client living with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder

Suicide Prevention

Defining Characteristics

A safety-focused concept that combines direct assessment, environmental safety, observation level, support-system involvement, and follow-up. Asking directly about suicidal thoughts does not increase risk.

Antecedents

  • Depression, hopelessness, grief, trauma, substance use, prior attempt, or major loss
  • Access to means of harm, limited support, or recent discharge/transition
  • Verbal or behavioral warning signs that must never be dismissed

Negative Consequences

Inadequate assessment, poor handoff, unsecured environment, or missed follow-up can place the client at acute risk.

Related Concepts

Crisis intervention, depression, grief, trauma, substance use, safety planning, therapeutic communication, and community prevention.

Exemplars

  • Primary: community education, stigma reduction, and routine screening
  • Secondary: direct suicide-risk assessment, precautions, observation, and environmental safety during acute risk
  • Tertiary: follow-up care, safety-plan review, family/support education, and relapse-prevention supports after crisis

Violence Escalation Cycle

Defining Characteristics

A predictable pattern of increasing anger, agitation, and aggression that can progress from anxiety and verbal escalation to physical risk if early de-escalation is not effective.

Antecedents

  • Frustration, fear, pain, intoxication, psychosis, trauma triggers, overstimulation, or limit setting
  • History of aggression, poor impulse control, or environmental crowding
  • Early cues such as pacing, loud voice, clenched fists, threats, or invading personal space

Negative Consequences

Escalation can lead to injury, trauma, emergency medication, seclusion/restraint, property damage, legal consequences, and loss of therapeutic trust.

Related Concepts

De-escalation, crisis intervention, milieu safety, seclusion/restraint, trauma-informed care, limit setting, and therapeutic communication.

Exemplars

  • Positive: nurse keeps a calm voice, offers space, states clear limits, gives choices, and removes excess stimulation before aggression occurs
  • Negative: power struggle, crowding the client, blocking exits, arguing, or delaying team support until the situation becomes unsafe
