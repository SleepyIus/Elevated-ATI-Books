Worked ExampleTherapeutic CommunicationCh 3 — Effective Communication · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Concept
Therapeutic Communication
Review Module Chapter
Ch 3 — Effective Communication

Related Content

Purposeful, client-centered, goal-directed communication (not social or reciprocal). It travels through verbal (vocabulary, intonation, clarity, pacing) and nonverbal (posture, facial expression, eye contact, silence) channels — and the nonverbal can carry more impact.

Underlying Principles

  • Establish trust and a nonjudgmental environment
  • Observe for congruence between verbal and nonverbal messages
  • Empathy keeps the nurse supportive yet objective

Nursing Interventions

Techniques: active listening (SOLER), open-ended questions, silence, restating, reflecting, clarifying, focusing, summarizing, and offering general leads.

Avoid these blocks: giving advice, false reassurance, "why" questions, minimizing feelings, changing the subject, and value judgments.

Worked ExampleDefense MechanismsCh 4 — Stress and Defense Mechanisms · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Concept
Defense Mechanisms
Review Module Chapter
Ch 4 — Stress and Defense Mechanisms

Related Content

Unconscious strategies that manage anxiety; they are reversible and can be adaptive or maladaptive. Only altruism and sublimation are always healthy.

Examples: denial, repression, suppression, displacement, projection, reaction formation, rationalization, regression, compensation, undoing, identification, and introjection.

Underlying Principles

  • Adaptive vs maladaptive is judged by frequency, intensity, duration, and reality testing
  • Using a variety of mechanisms is healthier than the same reaction to every stressor
  • Maladaptive when they interfere with functioning, relationships, or reality

Nursing Interventions

  • Identify the mechanisms the client is using
  • Do not strip away defenses abruptly
  • Help the client develop healthier coping strategies
  • Promote insight gradually and support reality testing
Worked ExampleEthical Principles in MH NursingCh 2 — Legal and Ethical Issues · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Concept
Ethical Principles in MH Nursing
Review Module Chapter
Ch 2 — Legal and Ethical Issues

Related Content

Five bioethical principles guide practice: beneficence (do good), autonomy (right to decide), justice (fair treatment), fidelity (loyalty), and veracity (truthfulness) — with nonmaleficence (do no harm).

Underlying Principles

  • Apply the principles to every ethical dilemma
  • Balance autonomy against safety
  • HIPAA governs confidentiality
  • Tort law separates intentional torts (assault, battery, false imprisonment) from unintentional (negligence, malpractice)

Nursing Interventions

  • Identify the ethical conflict and the competing principles
  • Involve the interprofessional team or ethics committee
  • Maintain veracity and fidelity with the client
  • Protect autonomy while ensuring safety; document the reasoning
Worked ExampleClient Rights and CommitmentCh 2 — Legal and Ethical Issues · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Concept
Client Rights and Commitment
Review Module Chapter
Ch 2 — Legal and Ethical Issues

Related Content

Mental health clients retain all civil rights plus protected treatment rights: informed consent, the right to refuse treatment, confidentiality, a written plan of care, communication with the outside, interpreter services, dignity, psychiatric advance directives, and care with the least restrictive interventions.

Admission ranges from voluntary to emergency and long-term involuntary commitment.

Underlying Principles

  • Care must be ethical, legal, and least restrictive
  • Involuntary commitment requires danger to self/others or grave disability, with due process
  • State laws vary and govern practice

Nursing Interventions

  • Verify consent and the client's competence
  • Honor refusal of treatment unless legal criteria override
  • Protect confidentiality — break only for duty to warn or mandated reporting
  • Advocate for least restrictive care; document thoroughly
  • Use seclusion/restraint only as a last resort with a provider order and frequent monitoring

Student Name
Concept
Therapeutic Milieu
Review Module Chapter
Ch 5 — Creating and Maintaining a Therapeutic and Safe Environment

Related Content

The managed therapeutic environment that supports the nurse-client relationship — safe, structured, consistent, confidential, and reliable — designed to minimize stress and maximize therapeutic benefit.

Underlying Principles

  • Physical and emotional safety are foundational
  • Structure and predictability reduce anxiety
  • The entire environment is therapeutic, not just formal sessions

Nursing Interventions

  • Ensure physical safety — no sharps/substances, monitored visitors, prevent elopement
  • Set clear, consistent expectations and use planned de-escalation
  • Hold community meetings; promote client responsibility within limits
Worked ExampleTherapeutic Nurse-Client RelationshipCh 5 — Creating and Maintaining a Therapeutic and Safe Environment · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Concept
Therapeutic Nurse-Client Relationship
Review Module Chapter
Ch 5 — Creating and Maintaining a Therapeutic and Safe Environment

Related Content

A goal-directed, time-limited, client-focused relationship with clear boundaries, unfolding in three phases: orientation, working, and termination.

Underlying Principles

  • Differs fundamentally from social or intimate relationships
  • Transference: client relates to the nurse as a significant past figure
  • Countertransference: nurse projects past feelings onto the client
  • Empathy is therapeutic; sympathy is not

Nursing Interventions

  • Orientation: build trust, set the contract/boundaries/goals
  • Working: problem-solve, build coping, address resistance and transference
  • Termination: review progress, plan, process feelings and possible regression
  • Maintain professional boundaries and self-awareness throughout

Student Name
Concept
Levels of Anxiety
Review Module Chapter
Ch 11 — Anxiety Disorders

Related Content

Anxiety progresses through four levels: mild (heightened perception and motivation), moderate (narrowed perception, learning still possible), severe (learning stops, focus on detail), and panic (loss of reality, disorganization).

Underlying Principles

  • As anxiety rises, the ability to learn and problem-solve falls
  • Nursing care is matched to the level of anxiety

Nursing Interventions

  • Mild/moderate: explore feelings and coping; open-ended questions
  • Severe/panic: stay with the client, low-stimulation environment, firm short directions, ensure safety
  • Defer health teaching until the acute anxiety subsides
Worked ExampleGrief and LossCh 27 — The Dying Client and Grief · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Concept
Grief and Loss
Review Module Chapter
Ch 27 — The Dying Client and Grief

Related Content

Grief is the response to loss. Types include anticipatory, normal/uncomplicated, complicated/maladaptive, and disenfranchised grief. Theories include Kübler-Ross's stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance), Bowlby, and Worden's tasks of mourning.

Underlying Principles

  • Grief is individual and nonlinear — stages need not occur in order
  • Culture and spirituality shape the experience
  • Complicated grief may require professional intervention

Nursing Interventions

  • Provide presence and active listening; encourage expression
  • Respect cultural and spiritual practices
  • Facilitate — never force — the grieving process
  • Support comfort and dignity for the dying client and family; identify complicated grief and refer

Student Name
Concept
Crisis and Crisis Intervention
Review Module Chapter
Ch 29 — Crisis Management

Related Content

A crisis occurs when usual coping fails; it is self-limiting (about 4–6 weeks). Types: maturational, situational, and adventitious.

Underlying Principles

  • Crisis intervention is short-term and present-focused
  • The goal is to return the client to the pre-crisis level of functioning
  • The nurse takes a more active, directive role than usual

Nursing Interventions

  • Assess safety first; identify the precipitating problem
  • Mobilize support systems and resources
  • Develop concrete, realistic coping steps
Worked ExampleStress and CopingCh 4 — Stress and Defense Mechanisms · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Concept
Stress and Coping
Review Module Chapter
Ch 4 — Stress and Defense Mechanisms

Related Content

Stress is the body's response to demand; eustress motivates while distress harms. Anxiety sits on a continuum: mild → moderate → severe → panic. The General Adaptation Syndrome moves through alarm, resistance, and exhaustion.

Underlying Principles

  • As anxiety rises, perception, learning, and problem-solving fall
  • Mild enhances perception; severe halts learning; panic loses touch with reality
  • Nursing care is matched to the anxiety level

Nursing Interventions

  • Mild/moderate: active listening, open-ended questions, explore coping
  • Severe/panic: stay with the client, low-stimulation environment, firm short statements, gross motor activity
  • Medications/restraints only after less restrictive measures fail
  • Teach relaxation, cognitive reframing, and assertiveness once calm
Worked ExampleAnxiety DisordersCh 11 — Anxiety Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Anxiety Disorders
Review Module Chapter
Ch 11 — Anxiety Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Excessive fear/worry that impairs function — GAD, panic disorder, phobias, OCD, and body dysmorphic disorder.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of GABA, serotonin, and norepinephrine with a heightened amygdala-driven fear response.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Stress management and sleep hygiene
  • Limit caffeine and stimulants
  • Early screening and intervention
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Family history; female sex
  • Chronic stress or trauma
  • Substance use; other anxiety/personality disorders

Expected Findings

  • GAD: excessive worry ≥ 6 months, restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance
  • Panic attack: palpitations, dyspnea, chest discomfort, fear of dying

Laboratory Tests

No specific test; rule out medical causes (thyroid, cardiac) as indicated.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and standardized anxiety rating scales.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Assess self-harm risk (especially in body dysmorphic disorder); ensure safety during panic.

Complications

Functional impairment, depression, substance use, and social isolation.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • During panic — stay with the client, remain calm and quiet, minimize stimuli
  • Use open-ended communication; avoid "why" questions and advice
  • Defer health teaching until the acute anxiety subsides

Medications

SSRIs/SNRIs (first-line, long-term), buspirone (GAD), benzodiazepines (short-term), hydroxyzine, and beta-blockers (situational).

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT, exposure/systematic desensitization, and relaxation training.

Client Education

  • Medications take weeks; do not stop abruptly
  • Practice relaxation and coping; limit caffeine

Interprofessional Care

Therapy, psychiatry, and support groups; primary care for medical rule-out.

Worked ExampleObsessive-Compulsive DisorderCh 11 — Anxiety Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder
Review Module Chapter
Ch 11 — Anxiety Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Recurrent obsessions (intrusive thoughts) and compulsions (repetitive behaviors performed to reduce anxiety).

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Serotonin dysregulation with involvement of cortico-striatal-thalamic circuits.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Early recognition
  • Stress reduction
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Family history
  • Onset often in childhood/early adulthood
  • Association with tics

Expected Findings

  • Time-consuming rituals (checking, washing, ordering, counting)
  • Marked distress if rituals are interrupted
  • Awareness that the thoughts are excessive

Laboratory Tests

None specific.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview; Y-BOCS severity scale.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Monitor for skin breakdown (e.g., excessive washing) and severe functional impairment; assess for depression and self-harm.

Complications

Depression and marked impairment in daily functioning.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Allow time for rituals initially; do not abruptly stop them
  • Set gradual limits; reduce anxiety to decrease compulsions
  • Provide structure and routine

Medications

SSRIs (often higher doses) and clomipramine.

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT with exposure and response prevention (ERP).

Client Education

Adherence to therapy; medications take several weeks; understand the rationale for ERP.

Interprofessional Care

Therapy plus psychiatry; family education.

Worked ExamplePost-Traumatic Stress DisorderCh 12 — Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder
Review Module Chapter
Ch 12 — Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Persistent symptoms > 1 month after a traumatic event — re-experiencing, avoidance, negative cognition/mood, and hyperarousal.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulated stress response (HPA axis), heightened amygdala reactivity, and altered fear conditioning.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Early psychological support after trauma
  • Debriefing and building coping/social support
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Severity/proximity of the trauma; prior trauma
  • Lack of support; combat or assault exposure

Expected Findings

  • Flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance, emotional numbing
  • Negative self-image, hypervigilance, exaggerated startle
  • Difficulty concentrating and sleeping

Laboratory Tests

None specific.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and trauma-specific screening tools.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Assess for suicidal ideation and self-harm, and ensure a safe, predictable environment.

Complications

Depression, substance use, relationship/occupational impairment, and suicidality.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Establish safety and trust; use trauma-informed care
  • Encourage expression at the client's own pace
  • Teach grounding techniques; avoid flooding with traumatic details

Medications

SSRIs/SNRIs (first-line); prazosin for nightmares; avoid long-term benzodiazepines.

Therapeutic Procedures

Trauma-focused CBT, EMDR, exposure therapy, and group support.

Client Education

  • Recovery takes time; practice coping and grounding
  • Adhere to medication; involve the support system

Interprofessional Care

Therapy, psychiatry, veteran/community resources, and support groups.

Worked ExampleMajor Depressive DisorderCh 13 — Depressive Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Major Depressive Disorder
Review Module Chapter
Ch 13 — Depressive Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

At least five findings (depressed mood, anhedonia, sleep/appetite change, fatigue, worthlessness, poor concentration) for ≥ 2 weeks.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, with neuroendocrine and genetic contributions.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Screening (e.g., PHQ-9)
  • Exercise, social connection, stress management
  • Treat comorbid medical illness
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Female sex; family history
  • Chronic medical illness; recent loss
  • Prior depression; substance use

Expected Findings

  • Depressed mood, anhedonia, psychomotor changes
  • Fatigue, sleep/appetite disturbance
  • Worthlessness/guilt, poor concentration, hopelessness

Laboratory Tests

No specific test; rule out medical causes (thyroid, anemia, vitamin deficiency).

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and depression rating scales (PHQ-9, Geriatric Depression Scale).

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Safety is the priority. Assess suicide risk directly; risk is greatest early in treatment and as energy returns before mood lifts. Use one-to-one observation when indicated and remove potentially harmful items from the environment.

Complications

Suicide, functional impairment, substance use, and a chronic or recurrent course.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Ensure safety; use simple, direct communication and allow time to respond
  • Promote ADLs, nutrition, and sleep; provide structure
  • Set small, achievable goals; spend time even in silence

Medications

SSRIs (first-line), SNRIs, atypicals (bupropion, mirtazapine), TCAs, MAOIs. Monitor for emerging suicidal ideation (black box); effect takes weeks.

Therapeutic Procedures

CBT, interpersonal therapy, ECT for severe/refractory cases, and light therapy (seasonal pattern).

Client Education

  • Medications take 2–4+ weeks; do not stop abruptly
  • Report worsening mood or suicidal thoughts
  • Understand the acute, continuation, and maintenance phases

Interprofessional Care

Therapy, psychiatry, social work, support groups, and crisis resources.

Worked ExampleBipolar DisordersCh 14 — Bipolar Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Bipolar Disorders
Review Module Chapter
Ch 14 — Bipolar Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Mood disorder cycling between mania/hypomania and depression: bipolar I (full mania), bipolar II (hypomania + depression), and cyclothymia.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin, with genetic and neurobiological contributions.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Sleep regulation and stress management
  • Medication adherence
  • Avoid stimulants and caffeine
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Family history
  • Onset in early adulthood
  • Substance use

Expected Findings

  • Mania: elevated/irritable mood, grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, risky behavior, possible psychosis
  • Depression: findings as in MDD

Laboratory Tests

Lithium level monitoring; renal and thyroid function; valproate levels and LFTs.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and mood charting; rule out medical/substance causes.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Monitor for escalating behavior (priority), exhaustion, and dehydration; assess suicide risk in the depressive phase; provide a low-stimulation environment.

Complications

Injury, exhaustion, suicide, and financial/legal consequences of risky behavior.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Set consistent, firm, concise limits; redirect energy
  • Low-stimulation environment; protect from exhaustion
  • High-calorie finger foods; decline gifts matter-of-factly

Medications

Lithium (first-line, narrow range), anticonvulsants (valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine), and atypical antipsychotics.

Therapeutic Procedures

Psychotherapy, psychoeducation, and ECT for severe or refractory mania.

Client Education

  • Take medications consistently — not only when symptomatic
  • Sleep changes can signal relapse; anhedonia signals depressive relapse
  • Lithium: maintain fluid/sodium intake and get regular monitoring; avoid caffeine

Interprofessional Care

Psychiatry, therapy, social work, and support groups.

Worked ExampleSchizophrenia / Psychotic DisordersCh 15 — Psychotic Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Schizophrenia / Psychotic Disorders
Review Module Chapter
Ch 15 — Psychotic Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Loss of contact with reality with positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech/behavior) and negative symptoms (flat affect, avolition, anhedonia, alogia, withdrawal).

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dopamine dysregulation (mesolimbic excess), with genetic, neurodevelopmental, and environmental contributions.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Early intervention and medication adherence
  • Stress reduction and family education
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Family history; onset late teens–early 20s
  • Prenatal/birth complications; cannabis use

Expected Findings

  • Hallucinations (often auditory) and delusions
  • Disorganized thought/speech (loose/clang associations, neologisms)
  • Negative symptoms; impaired insight (anosognosia)

Laboratory Tests

None diagnostic; baseline metabolic panel before antipsychotics; clozapine requires ANC monitoring.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview; rule out substance-induced or medical psychosis.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Assess for command hallucinations to harm self or others → one-to-one observation first; provide a calm, low-stimulation environment.

Complications

Self-harm/harm to others, medication nonadherence, relapse, social/occupational decline, and metabolic effects.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Ask directly about hallucinations and command content; focus on feelings
  • Do not argue about the reality of delusions
  • Consistent staff, build trust, reality orientation, promote adherence

Medications

Antipsychotics — typical (positive symptoms, EPS) and atypical (positive + negative, metabolic effects); long-acting injectables for adherence; clozapine for resistant cases (ANC monitoring).

Therapeutic Procedures

Social skills training, supportive therapy, psychoeducation, and Assertive Community Treatment (ACT).

Client Education

  • Long-term adherence — do not stop when feeling better
  • Report EPS and other side effects; attend metabolic monitoring
  • Recognize relapse signs

Interprofessional Care

Psychiatry, case management, ACT, family support, and vocational rehabilitation.

Worked ExamplePersonality DisordersCh 16 — Personality Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Personality Disorders
Review Module Chapter
Ch 16 — Personality Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Enduring, inflexible patterns that impair function, grouped into Cluster A (odd/eccentric), Cluster B (dramatic/erratic), and Cluster C (anxious/fearful).

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Genetic, neurobiological, and developmental/environmental factors (e.g., early trauma in borderline PD).

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Early intervention and skill-building
  • Consistent therapeutic relationships
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Family history
  • Childhood trauma, abuse, or neglect
  • Chaotic early environment

Expected Findings

  • Cluster A: paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal
  • Cluster B: antisocial (manipulation, no remorse), borderline (splitting, self-injury risk, unstable relationships), histrionic, narcissistic
  • Cluster C: avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive

Laboratory Tests

None specific.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview and assessment of longitudinal patterns.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

In borderline PD, assess self-harm/suicide risk and maintain safety with consistent limits.

Complications

Self-harm, substance use, relationship/occupational dysfunction, and comorbid depression/anxiety.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Communicate consistently to avoid being split
  • Use firm limit-setting for manipulation; assertiveness training for dependent/avoidant
  • Validate feelings while setting limits; maintain nurse self-awareness (countertransference)

Medications

No primary drug; treat comorbid symptoms (SSRIs, mood stabilizers, low-dose antipsychotics).

Therapeutic Procedures

Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) for borderline, plus CBT and group therapy.

Client Education

  • Adhere to therapy
  • Build coping and distress-tolerance skills

Interprofessional Care

Therapy and psychiatry with consistent team communication.

Worked ExampleNeurocognitive Disorders (Delirium & Dementia)Ch 17 — Neurocognitive Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Neurocognitive Disorders (Delirium & Dementia)
Review Module Chapter
Ch 17 — Neurocognitive Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Disrupted thinking, memory, and processing. Delirium is acute, fluctuating, and reversible; dementia is gradual, progressive, and irreversible.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Delirium: acute disturbance (infection, metabolic, drugs). Dementia (Alzheimer's): neuronal degeneration with amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Manage cardiovascular risk; cognitive and social engagement
  • Prevent/treat delirium causes; review medications
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Delirium: older age, infection, polypharmacy, surgery, dehydration
  • Dementia: age, family history, vascular disease

Expected Findings

  • Delirium: acute altered LOC, hallucinations, restlessness, fluctuating course
  • Dementia: gradual memory loss, the 4 A's (amnesia, aphasia, apraxia, agnosia), sundowning

Laboratory Tests

Delirium: CBC, electrolytes, urinalysis (UTI), drug levels to find the cause. Dementia: rule out reversible causes (B12, thyroid).

Diagnostic Procedures

MMSE/cognitive testing; neuroimaging for dementia; identify the delirium cause.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Home safety — locks high on exit doors, mattress on the floor, lighting over stairs, remove throw rugs, lock cleaning supplies; wandering precautions (ID bracelet, alarms); fall prevention.

Complications

Injury, falls, aspiration, infection, and caregiver burnout; untreated delirium leads to functional decline.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Delirium: identify and treat the cause, ensure safety, reorient
  • Dementia: introduce yourself, use simple positive statements, redirect (don't confront or ask "why")
  • Maintain routine and manage sundowning

Medications

Dementia: donepezil/cholinesterase inhibitors (bedtime, avoid NSAIDs) and memantine (moderate–severe); avoid anticholinergics; cautious antipsychotic use for severe agitation.

Therapeutic Procedures

Cognitive stimulation, reminiscence, structured routine, and caregiver support/respite.

Client Education

  • Caregiver education and respite resources; home safety
  • Medication teaching; delirium is reversible if the cause is treated

Interprofessional Care

Geriatrics, neurology, social work, home health, and caregiver support resources.

Worked ExampleSubstance Use and Addictive DisordersCh 18 — Substance Use and Addictive Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Substance Use and Addictive Disorders
Review Module Chapter
Ch 18 — Substance Use and Addictive Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Repeated substance use causing impairment over a 12-month period — loss of control, continued use despite problems, tolerance, withdrawal, and relapse.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Dysregulation of the dopaminergic reward pathway with neuroadaptation leading to tolerance and dependence.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Screening (CAGE) and education
  • Early intervention and relapse prevention
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Family history, mental illness, trauma
  • Peer/environmental influence; early use
  • Older adults after life-change stressors

Expected Findings

  • Alcohol withdrawal: tremors, vomiting, restlessness, tachycardia, hypertension
  • Opioid withdrawal: flu-like symptoms
  • Stimulant intoxication: agitation, tachycardia, dilated pupils

Laboratory Tests

Blood alcohol level, urine drug screen, LFTs, electrolytes; assess thiamine status in alcohol use.

Diagnostic Procedures

Screening tools (CAGE), clinical interview, and withdrawal assessment (CIWA).

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal → seizure precautions (priority); delirium tremens at 48–72 hr is an emergency; give thiamine before glucose to prevent Wernicke; monitor vital signs.

Complications

DTs/seizures, Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, overdose, relapse, and organ damage.

PATIENT-CENTERED CARE

Nursing Care

  • Manage safe withdrawal; use a nonjudgmental approach
  • Assess for codependency; promote abstinence
  • Support recovery and family involvement

Medications

Withdrawal: benzodiazepines (alcohol), carbamazepine (seizures), clonidine (opioid/autonomic). Abstinence: disulfiram, naltrexone, acamprosate. Opioid: methadone, buprenorphine, naloxone. Tobacco: NRT, varenicline, bupropion.

Therapeutic Procedures

12-step programs (AA), Al-Anon (family), CBT, motivational interviewing, and group therapy.

Client Education

  • Abstinence is the goal; address codependency
  • Disulfiram — avoid all alcohol-containing products
  • Relapse prevention and family involvement

Interprofessional Care

Addiction specialist, psychiatry, social work, and recovery programs.

Worked ExampleEating DisordersCh 19 — Eating Disorders · Open chapter ↗
Student Name
Disorder/Disease Process
Eating Disorders
Review Module Chapter
Ch 19 — Eating Disorders

Alterations in Health (Diagnosis)

Serious psychiatric conditions with high medical risk — anorexia nervosa (restriction, distorted body image, fear of weight gain), bulimia nervosa (binge-purge), and binge-eating disorder.

Pathophysiology Related to Client Problem

Multifactorial — neurobiological, genetic, psychological, and sociocultural, with serotonin involvement.

Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

  • Promote a healthy body image
  • Early screening; address cognitive distortions
ASSESSMENT

Risk Factors

  • Female sex; adolescence/young adulthood
  • Family history; perfectionism
  • Trauma; sociocultural pressures

Expected Findings

  • Anorexia: restriction, amenorrhea, lanugo, dry skin, bradycardia, cold intolerance
  • Bulimia: binge-purge cycles, near-normal weight, dental erosion, parotid swelling, Russell's sign
  • Cognitive distortions about food and body

Laboratory Tests

Electrolytes (hypokalemia from purging) and cardiac monitoring; assess for metabolic disturbance.

Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical interview, ECG (dysrhythmia risk), and electrolyte panel.

SAFETY

Safety Considerations

  • Screen for alcohol use disorder

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Ask the four questions nonjudgmentally
  • Combine results with a thorough history
  • Refer for further assessment when indicated

Interpretation of Findings

Two or more affirmative answers suggest problem drinking and warrant further assessment.

Potential Complications

Underreporting due to stigma; the tool is not diagnostic on its own.

Client Education

Honest answers guide care; emphasize a nonjudgmental, supportive approach.

Procedure Name
Suicide Risk Assessment & Prevention
Review Module Chapter
Ch 30 — The Client Who Is Suicidal

Description of Procedure

A structured assessment of suicide risk paired with protective interventions. Validated tools such as the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) standardize how clinicians ask about ideation, intensity, behavior, and lethality. Asking directly about suicidal thoughts does not increase risk.

Indications

  • Depression, hopelessness, or a prior attempt
  • Any verbal or behavioral warning signs
  • Routine screening in primary care, ED, and psychiatric settings

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Use a validated tool — e.g., the C-SSRS — to structure questions about ideation, plan, intent, and prior behavior
  • Ask directly and calmly; assess risk factors (prior attempt, hopelessness, loss, substance use) and protective factors
  • Never dismiss a verbal statement; document the client's own words
  • Determine the level of observation needed (e.g., one-to-one continuous observation for high risk)
  • Remove potentially harmful items from the environment and restrict access to means of harm
  • Collaborate on a safety plan; maintain a nonjudgmental, supportive presence

Interpretation of Findings

The C-SSRS classifies risk on a continuum — passive wishes for death → active ideation → active ideation with plan and intent → recent suicidal behavior — and this classification guides the intensity of safety measures. Risk can rise as energy returns before mood improves.

Potential Complications

Self-harm if risk is underestimated or the environment is not secured.

Client Education

Encourage open communication; involve trusted support; provide crisis resources and follow-up; reassure that help is available.

Sexual Assault & Survivor Care

Ch 33 — Sexual Assault

Description of Procedure

Coordinated, survivor-centered care after sexual assault — emotional support, optional forensic evidence collection by a SANE, and connection to resources. The survivor is never to blame.

Indications

  • A client disclosing recent or past sexual assault

Nursing Interventions (pre, intra, post)

  • Ensure safety and privacy; provide a nonjudgmental, supportive presence
  • Obtain informed consent before any forensic examination (SANE)
  • Preserve evidence per protocol if the survivor consents
  • Address injuries, pregnancy, and STI prophylaxis
  • Respect the survivor's choices and pace; offer advocacy and counseling resources

Interpretation of Findings

Recognize rape-trauma syndrome (acute disorganization moving toward longer-term reorganization); responses vary widely and are all valid.

Potential Complications

PTSD, depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation — assess and support.

Client Education

All responses are normal; the assault is not the survivor's fault; provide follow-up, counseling, and advocacy resources; explain options without pressure.
