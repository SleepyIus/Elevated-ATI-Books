Basic Concept - Ch 94

Moderate Sedation Readiness

A1AnesthesiaAirway first

Use this when a sedation question asks what must be ready before the procedure and what must be monitored until recovery.

Related Content

  • Moderate sedation keeps the client responsive enough to protect the airway while reducing pain and anxiety.
  • Common procedures include endoscopy, cardioversion, wound care, and minor procedures.

Underlying Principles

  • Older adults need closer monitoring because medication clearance can be reduced.
  • Airway, ventilation, blood pressure, level of consciousness, and capnography guide safety decisions.

Nursing Interventions

  • Verify consent, IV access, oxygen, suction, emergency equipment, and reversal agents.
  • Continue monitoring until presedation assessment findings return and discharge criteria are met.

Medication - Ch 94

Dantrolene for Malignant Hyperthermia

A7EmergencyRapid response

Use this when anesthesia suddenly triggers hypermetabolism, rigidity, rising carbon dioxide, dysrhythmias, or dangerous temperature elevation.

Purpose

  • Dantrolene is the priority medication used to stop the muscle-driven hypermetabolic crisis.
  • It belongs inside a coordinated malignant hyperthermia response, not as an isolated task.

Safety Monitoring

  • Stop the triggering anesthetic, support oxygenation, start cooling, and prepare intensive monitoring.
  • Trend vital signs, dysrhythmias, laboratory values, urine output, and organ-perfusion concerns.

Client Education

  • Teach the client and family that malignant hyperthermia risk is inherited and must be reported before future anesthesia.

Basic Concept - Ch 95

Preventing Surgical Complications

A1Preop safetyRisk reduction

Use this when the preoperative question asks which risks should be corrected or taught before the client reaches the operating room.

Related Content

  • Preop complications can stem from respiratory depression, fluid overload, infection, abnormal labs, medication effects, or thromboembolism risk.
  • Consent, medication review, skin/bowel preparation, and risk-factor screening are part of surgical readiness.

Underlying Principles

  • Uncontrolled blood glucose, elevated creatinine, or high WBCs can require provider notification before surgery.
  • Immobility and surgical stress increase clot risk, so prevention begins before the procedure.

Nursing Interventions

  • Clarify consent questions through the provider and witness only after the client understands.
  • Teach coughing, deep breathing, splinting, incentive spirometry, and ordered DVT prevention.

Nursing Skill - Ch 96

PACU Airway and Circulation Assessment

A9PACUABC priority

Use this when a newly arrived postoperative client needs immediate assessment after anesthesia handoff.

Indications

  • Every PACU transfer requires immediate full-body assessment with airway, breathing, and circulation first.
  • Aldrete scoring supports recovery readiness by tracking activity, consciousness, respiration, oxygen saturation, and circulation.

Steps

  • Assess airway patency, breath sounds, oxygenation, chest movement, vital-sign trends, bleeding, drains, and perfusion.
  • Position an unresponsive client laterally and keep rescue equipment available.

Report

  • Report worsening blood pressure trends, poor oxygenation, airway obstruction cues, excess bleeding, or urine output below expected thresholds.

System Disorder - Ch 96

Wound Dehiscence and Evisceration

A11Postop complicationDo not reinsert

Use this when the incision opens or abdominal contents protrude after surgery.

Pathophysiology

  • Dehiscence is wound separation; evisceration is protrusion of internal organs through the incision.
  • Risk rises with obesity, diabetes, poor nutrition, infection, coughing without splinting, hematoma, or steroid use.

Assessment

  • Watch for sudden wound opening, drainage changes, visible tissue, shock signs, pain, or client report that the incision gave way.

Nursing Care

  • Call for help, stay with the client, cover with sterile saline-moistened dressing, bend hips and knees, and notify the provider.
  • Do not push organs back into the wound.

Basic Concept - Ch 96

Postoperative Complication Sorting

A1Clinical judgmentPACU to unit

Use this when a question asks you to match the complication, likely cause, and first nursing response.

Related Content

  • Priority complications include airway obstruction, hypovolemia, paralytic ileus, DVT, and wound dehiscence/evisceration.
  • Risk factors include immobility, anemia, hypovolemia, respiratory disease, cardiac disease, diabetes, malnutrition, obesity, and age-related changes.

Underlying Principles

  • Low urine output, poor skin turgor, rising BUN, tachycardia, or concentrated labs can signal hypovolemia.
  • Absent bowel sounds after abdominal surgery can point toward paralytic ileus.

Nursing Interventions

  • Prioritize ABC changes first, then bleeding/perfusion, wound integrity, bowel function, urine output, and mobility/clot prevention.
  • Encourage ordered ambulation, coughing and deep breathing, splinting, and incentive spirometry while awake.

Basic Concept - Ch 82

Sick-Day Diabetes Safety

A1Unit 12DKA prevention

Use this when illness, poor intake, or GI losses create a hyperglycemia and ketone-monitoring risk.

Related Content

  • Illness can raise glucose even when the client is eating less.
  • Sick-day care protects hydration, glucose control, and early DKA detection.

Underlying Principles

  • Continue prescribed insulin or oral hypoglycemic therapy unless the provider gives a different plan.
  • Check glucose frequently during illness and test urine ketones when glucose is elevated, especially above the source threshold for ketone testing.

Nursing Interventions

  • Teach hydration and carbohydrate-containing fluids when solid food is not tolerated.
  • Escalate for moderate or large ketones, ketonuria lasting more than 24 hr, persistent glucose above 250 mg/dL, fever, confusion, rapid breathing, persistent GI losses, inability to tolerate liquids, or prolonged illness.

Medication - Ch 82

Insulin Timing and Mixing Safety

A7Unit 12Hypoglycemia prevention

Use this when a question asks how to safely time mealtime insulin or whether ordered insulins can be combined.

Purpose

  • Insulin therapy replaces basal and prandial insulin needs to control fasting, between-meal, and postprandial glucose.
  • Rapid-acting insulin is coordinated with meal availability to reduce hypoglycemia risk.

Medication Administration

  • Do not mix glargine or detemir with other insulin; administer in separate injections.
  • When mixing a rapid- or short-acting insulin with a longer-acting insulin that can be mixed, draw up the shorter-acting insulin first.

Nursing Interventions

  • Check glucose before insulin and monitor for sweating, weakness, dizziness, confusion, headache, tachycardia, or slurred speech at peak times.
  • Treat an awake client with hypoglycemia using rapid carbohydrate, then recheck glucose and follow with a longer snack if the next meal is delayed.

Basic Concept - Ch 83

DKA vs HHS Emergency Sorting

A1Unit 12Emergency priorities

Use this when severe hyperglycemia appears and the question asks whether the pattern fits DKA, HHS, or the first treatment priority.

Related Content

  • DKA has hyperglycemia with ketones and metabolic acidosis; HHS has profound hyperglycemia, hyperosmolar dehydration, and little to no ketosis.
  • DKA is closely linked with insufficient insulin; HHS is common in older adults who have type 2 diabetes and dehydration risk.

Underlying Principles

  • Osmotic diuresis causes dehydration and electrolyte shifts in both emergencies.
  • Insulin therapy and acidosis correction can shift potassium into cells, so potassium monitoring is a safety priority.

Nursing Interventions

  • Expect isotonic fluid replacement first, followed by IV regular insulin as prescribed and hourly glucose evaluation.
  • When glucose approaches 250 mg/dL, dextrose-containing fluid is added while treatment continues to reduce hypoglycemia and rapid osmolar-shift risk.

Basic Concept - Ch 31

ACS Recognition and First Priorities

A1Unit 4Chest pain

Use this when a chest-pain question asks whether findings fit stable angina, unstable angina, or myocardial infarction.

Related Content

  • Stable angina is triggered by exertion or stress and usually resolves with rest or nitroglycerin.
  • MI pain lasts longer, is not relieved by rest or nitroglycerin, and can include nausea, dyspnea, anxiety, diaphoresis, and epigastric distress.

Underlying Principles

  • Serial ECGs and cardiac markers help distinguish ischemia, injury, and infarction.
  • Any positive troponin indicates cardiac tissue damage and requires escalation.

Nursing Interventions

  • Monitor pain, vital signs, oxygenation, rhythm, serial ECGs, urine output, electrolytes, ABGs, and cardiac markers.
  • Reduce myocardial oxygen demand and support reperfusion-focused care as prescribed.

Medication - Ch 31

Nitroglycerin and Antiplatelet Safety

A7Unit 4Medication safety

Use this when a chest-pain medication question asks what to teach, monitor, or report.

Purpose

  • Nitroglycerin helps prevent coronary vasospasm and decreases preload and afterload, reducing myocardial oxygen demand.
  • Aspirin and clopidogrel reduce platelet aggregation that can contribute to coronary clot formation.

Safety Monitoring

  • Check for recent phosphodiesterase inhibitor use before nitroglycerin because severe hypotension can occur.
  • Monitor for bleeding and teach tinnitus reporting as a possible aspirin-toxicity cue.

Client Education

  • For chest pain, stop activity, rest, place nitroglycerin under the tongue, and call emergency services if pain is not relieved after 5 min.
  • Use additional nitroglycerin doses 5 min apart only as directed.

Basic Concept - Ch 32

Pulmonary Edema Rescue Priorities

A1Unit 4Oxygenation

Use this when heart failure suddenly becomes severe dyspnea, crackles, orthopnea, or pink frothy sputum.

Related Content

  • Pulmonary edema is life-threatening fluid accumulation in alveoli and interstitial lung spaces.
  • Expected findings include tachypnea, dyspnea, orthopnea, hypoxemia, crackles, cough, reduced urine output, confusion, S3, and anxiety.

Underlying Principles

  • High-Fowler's with legs dependent decreases venous return and supports ventilation.
  • Diuretics, vasodilators, morphine, inotropes, and oxygen/positive airway pressure are used as prescribed to reduce congestion and support cardiac output.

Nursing Interventions

  • Administer high-flow oxygen as prescribed and prepare for positive airway pressure, intubation, or ventilation if needed.
  • Monitor vital signs, breath sounds, oxygen saturation, ABGs, potassium, hemodynamics, intake/output, and hourly urine output.

Basic Concept - Ch 28

Dysrhythmia Treatment Sorting

A1Unit 4Pulse and perfusion

Use this when a rhythm question asks whether the client needs medication support, synchronized cardioversion, defibrillation, CPR, or pacing.

Related Content

  • Treatment depends on rhythm, symptoms, pulse, and perfusion.
  • Symptomatic bradycardia, atrial dysrhythmias, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and pulseless rhythms have different first-response pathways.

Underlying Principles

  • Symptomatic bradycardia can need atropine, vasoactive infusion support if atropine is ineffective, and pacemaker support.
  • Ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia requires rapid defibrillation and emergency medication support.

Nursing Interventions

  • Assess the client, not just the strip: level of consciousness, blood pressure, oxygenation, pulse, chest pain, and perfusion drive urgency.
  • Follow emergency protocols, call for help early, and prepare the ordered rhythm-specific treatment.

Therapeutic Procedure - Ch 28

Cardioversion vs Defibrillation Safety

A13Unit 4Shock safety

Use this when the question asks which electrical treatment fits the rhythm and what the nurse must do before and after shock delivery.

Indications

  • Synchronized cardioversion is used for selected symptomatic atrial dysrhythmias, supraventricular tachycardia, and ventricular tachycardia with a pulse.
  • Defibrillation is unsynchronized and is used for ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia.

Steps

  • For cardioversion, confirm synchronization, sedation/oxygenation as prescribed, emergency equipment, and a lead with an upright QRS complex.
  • Before any shock, all staff must clear the client, bed, and connected equipment.

Complications

  • Failure to synchronize cardioversion can trigger a lethal dysrhythmia.
  • After cardioversion or defibrillation, monitor airway, vital signs, ECG, perfusion, and skin under electrodes.

System Disorder - Ch 37

Shock Differentiation and First Actions

A11Unit 4Perfusion emergency

Use this when the client is deteriorating and the question asks which shock type is most likely or which cause-specific action comes first.

Pathophysiology

  • Cardiogenic shock is pump failure, hypovolemic shock is volume loss, obstructive shock is a noncardiac block to effective pumping, and distributive shock involves vasodilation and capillary leak.
  • Distributive shock includes septic, anaphylactic, and neurogenic patterns.

Assessment

  • Watch for altered mentation, dyspnea, hypoxia, cool or flushed skin patterns, weak pulses, narrowed pulse pressure, tachycardia, tachypnea, and reduced urine output.
  • Match clues to cause: volume loss, infection, allergen exposure, spinal/sympathetic loss, pump failure, or obstruction.

Nursing Care

  • Support airway and oxygenation, maintain IV access, monitor hemodynamics and urine output, and escalate promptly.
  • Use cause-specific priorities: volume replacement for hypovolemia, antibiotics for septic shock, epinephrine for anaphylaxis, and hemodynamic medication support as prescribed.

Basic Concept - Ch 19

Oxygen Delivery and Ventilator Priorities

A1Unit 3Oxygenation

Use this when a question asks how to match oxygen delivery, recognize ventilator risk, or decide whether weaning is failing.

Related Content

  • Choose the oxygen device that matches the client's needed FiO2 and titrate to the prescribed saturation.
  • Mechanical ventilation supports gas exchange when the client cannot maintain airway patency, oxygenation, or ventilation.

Underlying Principles

  • Venturi masks deliver precise FiO2, simple masks need enough flow to reduce CO2 rebreathing, and nonrebreathers require the reservoir bag to stay inflated.
  • PEEP keeps alveoli open at end expiration, improves oxygenation, and can increase pressure-related risk.

Nursing Interventions

  • Assess the client and the circuit before responding to ventilator alarms; low pressure can mean disconnection or leak, and high pressure can mean secretions, kinks, coughing, bronchospasm, pulmonary edema, or pneumothorax.
  • Watch weaning for tachypnea, desaturation, dysrhythmias, anxiety, diaphoresis, decreasing tidal volume, or declining level of consciousness.

System Disorder - Ch 25

Tension Pneumothorax Emergency Response

A11Unit 3Pleural emergency

Use this when chest trauma or sudden pleural pressure threatens ventilation and perfusion.

Pathophysiology

  • Air trapped in the pleural space can collapse lung tissue and, when pressure rises, shift mediastinal structures and reduce venous return.
  • Hemothorax and flail chest also impair lung expansion and can progress to respiratory failure.

Assessment

  • Watch for tachypnea, tachycardia, hypoxia, cyanosis, dyspnea, accessory muscle use, asymmetric chest movement, absent breath sounds, hyperresonance, subcutaneous emphysema, or tracheal deviation away from the affected side.

Nursing Care

  • Administer oxygen, position to maximize ventilation, monitor vital signs and SaO2, and prepare for emergency decompression or chest tube therapy as prescribed.
  • After chest tube insertion, monitor drainage, dressing, tube placement, drainage-system function, respiratory status, and chest x-ray/ABG trends.

System Disorder - Ch 26

ARDS and Respiratory Failure Safety

A11Unit 3ABG-driven care

Use this when hypoxemia persists, ABGs worsen, or ventilator support introduces pressure and tube risks.

Pathophysiology

  • Respiratory failure can be ventilatory failure, oxygenation failure, or both.
  • ARDS causes inflammatory injury to the alveolar-capillary membrane, reduced surfactant, stiff lungs, and severe hypoxemia even with high oxygen support.

Assessment

  • Trend dyspnea, orthopnea, rapid shallow breathing, cyanosis or mottling, tachycardia, hypotension, retractions, SaO2 below expected range, adventitious sounds, dysrhythmias, confusion, lethargy, ABGs, and chest x-ray changes.

Nursing Care

  • Maintain a patent airway, monitor respiratory status at least hourly, oxygenate before suctioning, and use prescribed PEEP or CPAP to prevent alveolar collapse.
  • Monitor for pneumothorax, aspiration pneumonia, tube displacement, infection, decreased cardiac output, hypotension, low urine output, and barotrauma.

Nursing Skill - Ch 40

Suspected Transfusion Reaction Response

A9Unit 5Stop first

Use this when symptoms begin during or soon after a blood product transfusion.

Indications

  • Acute hemolytic reactions can occur immediately after a small amount of incompatible blood.
  • Febrile, allergic, bacterial, and circulatory-overload reactions can appear during or after transfusion and require rapid differentiation.

Steps

  • Stop the transfusion for suspected reaction and maintain IV access with 0.9% sodium chloride using new tubing.
  • Monitor vital signs and fluid status, notify the provider, and send blood products/tubing or cultures as prescribed.

Report

  • Report fever/chills, low back pain, hypotension, chest tightness, dyspnea, itching, urticaria, anxiety, JVD, crackles, hemoglobinuria, or shock cues.

Basic Concept - Ch 40

Transfusion Reaction Sorting

A1Unit 5Reaction cues

Use this when the question asks which reaction pattern fits the client's findings.

Related Content

  • Acute hemolytic reaction is tied to incompatibility; febrile reaction is linked with anti-WBC antibodies; allergic reaction is sensitivity to a blood-product component.
  • Bacterial reaction suggests contaminated product, while overload reflects a rate or volume the client cannot tolerate.

Underlying Principles

  • The first 15 min of packed RBC transfusion is a high-risk window for acute hemolytic reaction.
  • Older adults and clients with cardiac or renal problems need close overload surveillance.

Nursing Interventions

  • Use 0.9% sodium chloride with blood products and never add medications to blood.
  • Match medications and positioning to the reaction: antihistamines for mild allergic reaction, antipyretics for febrile reaction, antibiotics/cultures for bacterial concern, and upright positioning plus oxygen/diuretic support for overload as prescribed.

Medication - Ch 42

DIC and HIT Anticoagulation Safety

A7Unit 5Clot and bleed

Use this when a coagulation question asks why anticoagulation can appear in a bleeding-risk disorder.

Purpose

  • DIC can create widespread microclots while consuming clotting factors, so the client can clot and bleed at the same time.
  • Heparin can be prescribed in DIC to reduce microclot formation and slow clotting-factor consumption.

Safety Monitoring

  • Trend platelets, fibrinogen, PT/PTT, thrombin time, fibrin split products, D-dimer, bleeding, perfusion, and organ-function cues.
  • HIT is associated with heparin exposure and a falling platelet count; direct thrombin inhibitors can be used instead when prescribed.

Client Education

  • Use bleeding precautions, avoid unnecessary needles and NSAIDs, avoid Valsalva, and report nose/gum bleeding, petechiae, ecchymosis, hematuria, or severe headache.

Diagnostic Procedure - Ch 56

Excretory Urography Contrast Safety

A3Unit 8Kidney protection

Use this when a renal diagnostic question combines iodine contrast, kidney reserve, and diabetes medication safety.

Description

  • Excretory urography uses iodine-based IV contrast to detect obstruction, parenchymal masses, and kidney size changes.
  • Contrast can cause acute kidney injury, especially for older adults, dehydration, prior renal insufficiency, or nephrotoxic medication exposure.

Preprocedure

  • Encourage fluids the day before, complete prescribed bowel cleansing, keep the client NPO after midnight, and assess iodine/seafood/egg/milk/chocolate allergy or asthma.
  • Check BUN and creatinine and withhold metformin for 24 hr before the procedure when prescribed because iodine contrast raises lactic-acidosis risk.

Postprocedure

  • Give IV or oral fluids as prescribed to flush contrast through the renal system.
  • Recheck BUN and creatinine before restarting metformin and monitor for contrast-related kidney injury.

Therapeutic Procedure - Ch 57

Hemodialysis Access and Postprocedure Safety

A13Unit 8Access first

Use this when a hemodialysis question asks what must be protected before, during, or after treatment.

Indications

  • Hemodialysis can support clients with AKI, CKD, persistent hyperkalemia, medication or illicit drug toxicity, or hypervolemia that does not respond to diuretics.
  • Dialysis removes excess fluid, electrolytes, and waste and helps restore acid-base balance, but it does not restore kidney or hormonal function.

Before/During

  • Check consent, baseline weight, vital signs, BUN, creatinine, electrolytes, hematocrit, and medications to hold.
  • Assess AV fistula or graft for bruit, palpable thrill, distal pulses, and circulation; do not use the access arm for blood pressure, injections, venipuncture, or IV catheters.

After

  • Compare pre- and postdialysis weight, check labs and vital signs, and monitor bleeding, infection, hypovolemia, muscle cramps, headache, and disequilibrium findings.
  • Teach the client to report bleeding longer than 30 min, absent thrill/bruit, or access-site infection cues.

Diagnostic Procedure - Ch 57

Peritoneal Dialysis Complication Sorting

A3Unit 8Cloudy outflow

Use this when a peritoneal dialysis question asks which complication pattern fits the outflow, access site, or metabolic findings.

Description

  • Peritoneal dialysis instills hypertonic dialysate into the peritoneal cavity, allows dwell time, then drains fluid that contains waste products.
  • Maintain surgical asepsis when accessing the catheter and keep the outflow bag below the abdomen for gravity drainage.

Complications

  • Cloudy or opaque effluent is an early peritonitis cue; monitor fever, pain, purulent drainage, redness, swelling, and discolored dialysate.
  • Protein loss, hyperglycemia, access-site infection or leakage, respiratory distress, abdominal pain, and poor inflow/outflow can occur.

Nursing Interventions

  • Monitor glucose because dialysate contains glucose, increase dietary protein as prescribed, and monitor albumin.
  • For poor flow, reposition the client, check kinks or clamps, milk tubing for fibrin clots as prescribed, and prevent constipation.

Basic Concept - Ch 43

Fluid Volume Deficit vs Excess

A1Unit 6Sort the pattern

Use this when a question asks whether findings point to dehydration/hypovolemia or fluid overload/hypervolemia.

Related Content

  • Fluid volume deficit is loss of water plus electrolytes with decreased circulating volume.
  • Fluid volume excess is too much water and electrolytes, often from impaired regulation or sodium-driven retention.

Assessment

  • Deficit cues include tachycardia, thready pulse, orthostatic hypotension, oliguria, dry mucous membranes, poor turgor, concentrated urine, elevated hematocrit/BUN, and high urine specific gravity.
  • Excess cues include weight gain, edema, bounding pulse, hypertension, JVD, crackles, dyspnea, dilutional labs, and pulmonary edema risk.

Nursing Interventions

  • For deficit, rehydrate as prescribed, monitor I&O and orthostatic vital signs, trend mentation, and use fall precautions.
  • For excess, monitor daily weight, I&O, lung sounds, edema, sodium/potassium, and maintain prescribed sodium/fluid restrictions.

System Disorder - Ch 44

Severe Hyponatremia Neurologic Safety

A11Unit 6Seizure risk

Use this when low sodium is paired with confusion, seizures, or other neurologic deterioration.

Pathophysiology

  • Hyponatremia reflects sodium loss or water gain, causing water to shift into cells and slowing depolarization.
  • Severe or acute hyponatremia can progress to coma, seizures, respiratory arrest, and death if not treated promptly.

Assessment

  • Monitor headache, confusion, lethargy, fatigue, weakness, decreased DTRs, dizziness, GI cramping, nausea, seizures, vital signs, I&O, daily weight, and level of consciousness.

Nursing Care

  • Implement seizure precautions and support airway safety for severe neurologic findings.
  • Administer sodium replacement as prescribed; 3% sodium chloride is given slowly with frequent sodium checks because rapid sodium correction is dangerous.

Medication - Ch 44

Potassium Replacement and Hyperkalemia Rescue

A7Unit 6Rhythm first

Use this when potassium therapy or high potassium creates cardiac-rhythm risk.

Purpose

  • Potassium supports nerve impulses, skeletal/respiratory muscle, and cardiac tissue, so small changes can become high-risk.
  • Hypokalemia requires safe replacement; severe hyperkalemia requires therapies that stabilize the heart, shift potassium into cells, or remove potassium.

Safety Monitoring

  • Never give potassium by IV bolus, IM, or subcutaneous route; monitor urine output, cardiac rhythm, respirations, oxygen saturation, bowel sounds, hand grasps, DTRs, and IV-site phlebitis.
  • With hyperkalemia, stop potassium infusions/supplements, maintain IV access, restrict potassium as prescribed, monitor I&O and potassium, and watch for hypokalemia during treatment.

Rescue Therapies

  • Severe hyperkalemia can require calcium gluconate, dextrose with regular insulin, sodium bicarbonate for acidosis, loop diuretics if kidney function is adequate, potassium binders, beta-2 agonists, or dialysis as prescribed.

Diagnostic Procedure - Ch 46

GI Endoscopy Prep and Recovery

A3Unit 7Prep to recovery

Use this when a GI diagnostic question asks what to verify before a scope or what to monitor after sedation, biopsy, or contrast.

Description

  • GI scope procedures can visualize bleeding, ulceration, inflammation, polyps, tumors, biliary structures, or the small bowel depending on the procedure.
  • Colonoscopy requires bowel prep and commonly uses moderate sedation; EGD and ERCP require airway and gag-reflex safety after the procedure.

Preprocedure

  • Verify consent, allergies, baseline labs, vital signs, recent intake, medication restrictions, prior barium/contrast studies, bowel prep, and NPO status.
  • Check medication instructions because aspirin, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, warfarin, and some bowel preps can affect safety.

Postprocedure

  • Monitor vital signs, respiratory status, airway, bleeding, severe pain, fever, abdominal/chest pain, and perforation or aspiration cues.
  • Withhold oral fluids after EGD or ERCP until the gag reflex returns; after barium studies, increase fluids and monitor contrast elimination as prescribed.

System Disorder - Ch 52

IBD Flare and Complication Safety

A11Unit 7Escalate early

Use this when a bowel inflammation question asks whether the pattern fits ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, diverticulitis, or a dangerous complication.

Pathophysiology

  • Ulcerative colitis primarily affects the rectum/rectosigmoid colon and can cause bloody, mucus-filled stools and left-lower-quadrant cramping.
  • Crohn's disease can affect the GI tract from mouth to anus, commonly involves the distal ileum, and can cause right-lower-quadrant cramping, fistulas, malabsorption, and steatorrhea.

Assessment

  • Trend stool count and blood, I&O, weight, dehydration cues, potassium/electrolytes, Hgb/Hct, WBC, ESR/CRP, abdominal distention, fever, and pain location.
  • Escalate rigid abdomen, rebound tenderness, fever, tachycardia, severe pain, vomiting, hypotension, absent/decreased bowel sounds, or older-adult confusion.

Nursing Care

  • During active disease, teach small frequent high-protein, high-calorie, low-fiber meals, adequate fluids, caffeine/alcohol avoidance, and vitamin/iron supplementation as prescribed.
  • Severe diverticulitis or toxic megacolon can require NPO status, NG suction, IV fluids/electrolytes, antibiotics or corticosteroids, and surgery preparation.

Medication - Ch 52

IBD Medication Safety Map

A7Unit 7Monitor toxicity

Use this when a medication question asks which labs, adverse effects, or client teaching fit IBD therapies.

Purpose

  • 5-ASA medications reduce intestinal mucosal inflammation; corticosteroids induce remission but are not for long-term control.
  • Immunosuppressants and immunomodulators are reserved for more severe or refractory disease and raise infection or bleeding concerns.

Safety Monitoring

  • For sulfasalazine, monitor CBC, kidney and hepatic function, and report sore throat, rash, bruising, fever, or infection cues.
  • For mesalamine-class therapy, monitor kidney toxicity; for corticosteroids, monitor BP, electrolytes, glucose, infection, and fluid retention.

Client Education

  • Teach water intake and sun protection with sulfasalazine, slow tapering of corticosteroids, and infection avoidance with immunosuppressive therapy.
  • Use antidiarrheals cautiously because toxic megacolon can develop; report fever, hypotension, abdominal distention, or decreased bowel sounds.
