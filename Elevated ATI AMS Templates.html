Asthma Rescue and Control Medication Safety

A7Unit 3Rescue vs control

Use this when an asthma question asks which inhaler relieves acute symptoms, how controller therapy prevents inflammation, or when severe worsening becomes an emergency.

Purpose

  • Short-acting beta2 agonists such as albuterol provide rapid relief of acute asthma manifestations and can be used before exercise when prescribed.
  • Anti-inflammatory agents, including inhaled or systemic corticosteroids, reduce airway inflammation and support long-term control rather than immediate rescue.

Safety Monitoring

  • Monitor oxygen saturation, work of breathing, wheezing, accessory muscle use, and response after bronchodilator therapy.
  • With systemic corticosteroids, watch for hyperglycemia, fluid retention, poor wound healing, and decreased immune response.

Client Education

  • If separate inhaled bronchodilator and anti-inflammatory products are scheduled together, use the bronchodilator first to improve delivery of the anti-inflammatory medication.
  • Teach the client to seek emergency care for red-zone peak flow or severe symptoms that do not respond to prescribed reliever medication.

System Disorder - Ch 22

COPD Breathing, Bronchodilator, and Home Safety

A11Unit 3Airflow support

Use this when a COPD question asks how to reduce dyspnea, evaluate inhaler teaching, or protect a client who uses oxygen at home.

Expected Findings

  • COPD assessment can include chronic dyspnea, productive morning cough, hypoxemia, crackles or wheezes, rapid shallow respirations, cyanosis, and reduced oxygen saturation.
  • Acute infection or worsening respiratory status can require prompt follow-up and possible hospital-level care.

Nursing Interventions

  • Teach pursed-lip breathing by inhaling through the nose, exhaling through pursed lips without puffing the cheeks, and using the technique to control breathing.
  • Reinforce smoking cessation, avoiding secondhand smoke and irritants, prescribed oxygen use, and infection prevention through hand hygiene and immunizations.

Medication Safety

  • Short-acting beta2 agonists such as albuterol provide rapid relief but can cause tachycardia.
  • Ipratropium-type anticholinergic therapy supports bronchodilation and secretion reduction; monitor for dry mouth, headache, blurred vision, palpitations, and heart-rate changes.

System Disorder - Ch 23

Tuberculosis Transmission and Precautions

A11Unit 3Airborne safety

Use this when a tuberculosis question asks how to prevent transmission, what isolation is needed, or how to handle transport and sputum disposal.

Pathophysiology

  • Tuberculosis is transmitted through airborne aerosolization and primarily affects the lungs, though it can spread through the blood.
  • Expected findings can include a persistent cough longer than 3 weeks, purulent or blood-streaked sputum, fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, night sweats, and low-grade afternoon fever.

Nursing Interventions

  • For hospitalized clients, wear an N95 or HEPA respirator, place the client in a negative-airflow room, and implement airborne precautions.
  • If transport is necessary, have the client wear a surgical mask and use the shortest, least busy route.

Teaching

  • Teach the client to cough and expectorate sputum into tissues disposed of in provided bags or no-touch receptacles.
  • Family members and exposed contacts should be tested for TB; home airborne precautions are not typically needed because household members have already been exposed.

Medication - Ch 23

Tuberculosis Medication Adherence and Safety

A7Unit 3Resistance prevention

Use this when a tuberculosis medication question asks why multiple drugs are prescribed, what adverse effects to report, or how to support long-course adherence.

Purpose

  • Combination therapy is recommended because resistant tuberculosis strains can develop when therapy is incomplete or inconsistent.
  • A typical regimen includes isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol.

Safety Monitoring

  • With isoniazid, monitor liver function and hepatotoxicity cues such as jaundice, anorexia, malaise, fatigue, or nausea; pyridoxine can be prescribed to reduce neurotoxicity risk.
  • Rifampin can turn urine and other secretions orange; ethambutol requires vision-change reporting because optic neuritis can occur.

Client Education

  • Complete the full prescribed medication course to eliminate bacteria and reduce resistance risk.
  • Continue follow-up; sputum samples are used every 2 to 4 weeks to monitor therapy effectiveness until the client is no longer infectious.

System Disorder - Ch 24

Pulmonary Embolism Recognition and Priority Care

A11Unit 3Oxygenation first

Use this when a pulmonary embolism question asks which client is at risk, which cues fit PE, or what action comes first when oxygenation is threatened.

Pathophysiology

  • A pulmonary embolism blocks pulmonary vasculature and can impair blood flow and oxygenation; venous thromboembolism is the most common source.
  • Risk rises with long immobility, pregnancy or estrogen therapy, tobacco use, obesity, orthopedic/pelvic surgery, central venous catheters, chronic atrial fibrillation, cancer, trauma, long-bone fracture, septicemia, and advanced age.

Assessment

  • Watch for anxiety or impending doom, sudden chest pressure or pain with inspiration, dyspnea, air hunger, cough, hemoptysis, pleural friction rub, tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnea, crackles, diaphoresis, low-grade fever, low oxygen saturation, cyanosis, petechiae, JVD, or syncope.
  • ABG results can begin with low PaCO2 from hyperventilation and worsen as hypoxemia progresses; D-dimer can rise with clot formation.

Nursing Care

  • Administer oxygen and position high-Fowler to maximize ventilation when respiratory status is the priority.
  • Maintain IV access, give prescribed medications, assess respiratory status at least every 30 min, monitor cardiac rhythm and perfusion, and provide calm support while care escalates.

Medication - Ch 24

Pulmonary Embolism Anticoagulation and Procedure Safety

A7Unit 3Bleeding risk

Use this when a PE treatment question asks how anticoagulants differ from thrombolytics, what to monitor, or when procedure support might be needed.

Purpose

  • Unfractionated heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin, enoxaparin, and warfarin prevent existing clots from enlarging and help prevent additional clots.
  • Thrombolytic therapy, including alteplase, reteplase, or tenecteplase, dissolves clots to restore pulmonary blood flow when prescribed.

Safety Monitoring

  • Assess for bleeding contraindications such as active bleeding, peptic ulcer disease, history of stroke, recent trauma or surgery, uncontrolled hypertension, pregnancy, or known bleeding disorders as applicable to the therapy.
  • Monitor PT/INR for warfarin, aPTT for heparin, CBC, oxygen saturation, vital signs, thrombocytopenia, anemia, hemorrhage, bruising, and occult blood.

Teaching and Procedures

  • Teach consistent vitamin K intake with warfarin, scheduled blood draws, bleeding precautions, soft toothbrush/electric razor use, and avoidance of aspirin products unless prescribed.
  • Prepare for embolectomy or vena cava filter insertion when ordered; both require consent/NPO preparation and postoperative monitoring of vital signs, oxygen saturation, incision drainage, and pain.

Diagnostic Procedure - Ch 17

Respiratory Diagnostic Procedure Safety

A3Unit 3Procedure prep

Use this when a respiratory procedure question asks what to do before or after PFTs, ABGs, bronchoscopy, or thoracentesis.

Description

  • PFTs evaluate lung function, airflow, gas exchange, and airway resistance; ABGs evaluate oxygenation and acid-base status.
  • Bronchoscopy visualizes the airway and can obtain samples; thoracentesis removes fluid or air from the pleural space and can obtain specimens.

Nursing Actions

  • For PFTs, reinforce no smoking for 6 to 8 hr before testing and clarify prescribed inhaler-hold timing.
  • For ABGs, verify collateral circulation before radial puncture, use a heparinized syringe, send the sample promptly on ice, and hold pressure after puncture.

Complications

  • After bronchoscopy, withhold oral intake until gag/cough reflex returns and report bronchospasm, significant hemoptysis, hypoxemia, or persistent fever.
  • For thoracentesis, position upright over the bedside table, limit fluid removal as prescribed, monitor respiratory status, and report dyspnea, fever, hypotension, or pneumothorax cues.

Therapeutic Procedure - Ch 18

Chest Tube Monitoring and Emergency Response

A13Unit 3Water seal

Use this when a chest tube question asks whether a drainage-system finding is expected or how to respond to disconnection, removal, or tension pneumothorax risk.

Description

  • Chest tubes drain air, blood, or fluid from the pleural space, restore negative pressure, and support lung expansion.
  • The three-chamber system includes drainage collection, water seal, and wet or dry suction control.

Nursing Actions

  • Keep the drainage system upright, below chest level, and with tubing straight enough to drain by gravity; monitor drainage amount, color, water seal level, suction, tidaling, and bubbling.
  • Keep oxygen, sterile water, enclosed hemostats, and an occlusive dressing at the bedside.

Complications

  • Continuous bubbling in the water seal suggests an air leak; assess connections and notify the provider if an air leak persists.
  • If the drainage system is compromised, place the tube end in sterile water; if the chest tube is accidentally removed, cover the site with dry sterile gauze and escalate.

Health Risk and Adherence Support

A1Unit 1Risk reduction

Use this when a foundations question asks which health variables can change or which nursing actions improve adherence after a new diagnosis.

Related Content

  • Health and wellness are individualized and influenced by modifiable variables such as smoking, nutrition, access to health education, sexual practices, exercise, and treatable conditions.
  • Nonmodifiable variables include sex, age, developmental level, and genetic traits.

Underlying Principles

  • Health promotion and disease prevention use education and awareness to reduce risks and improve wellness.
  • Adherence can be limited by illness perceptions, trust in the provider, belief in therapy, culture or religion, support systems, family roles, cost, and access to care.

Nursing Interventions

  • Assess obstacles to adherence, identify support systems, and build strategies that reduce the client's specific barriers.
  • Use resources such as dietitian support, medication organizers, home education materials, support groups, smoking cessation, weight reduction, exercise teaching, and immunization counseling as indicated.

Basic Concept - Ch 2

ED Triage and Primary Survey Priorities

A1Unit 1Emergency first look

Use this when an emergency question asks how to sort severity, what to assess first, or when to escalate rapidly.

Related Content

  • The five triage levels are resuscitation, emergent, urgent, less urgent, and nonurgent.
  • The primary survey is a rapid, systematic assessment for life-threatening problems using ABCDE.

Underlying Principles

  • Airway and cervical spine protection come first; without a patent airway, later steps are ineffective.
  • Rapid response teams are used for early recognition and response when a client shows indications of rapid decline before arrest or stroke occurs.

Nursing Interventions

  • Use head-tilt/chin-lift for an unresponsive client without suspected trauma; use modified jaw thrust when cervical spine injury is possible.
  • For toxic ingestion, support airway and circulation, consider activated charcoal or gastric lavage/aspiration when ordered, avoid induced vomiting or ipecac, and provide IV fluids as indicated.

Medication - Ch 74

Psoriasis and Dermatitis Medication Safety

A7Unit 11Skin safety

Use this when skin-disorder treatment questions ask what to teach about topical steroids, vitamin D analogs, PUVA, antihistamines, or topical immunosuppressants.

Purpose

  • Psoriasis therapy reduces manifestations and slows epidermal cell turnover; dermatitis therapy reduces inflammation, pruritus, and exposure-triggered irritation.
  • Topical corticosteroids, vitamin D analogs, PUVA, antihistamines, tacrolimus, and pimecrolimus each carry product-specific precautions.

Safety Monitoring

  • Monitor high-potency topical corticosteroids for thinning, striae, hypopigmentation, and excess use on the face or skin folds.
  • Monitor calcipotriene-type therapy for irritation and hypercalcemia cues such as muscle weakness, fatigue, or anorexia.

Client Education

  • For PUVA, expect psoralen before UVA therapy and wear eye protection during treatment and for 24 hr afterward.
  • For pimecrolimus or tacrolimus, avoid occlusive dressings, stop when the rash clears, avoid use with infection, and avoid direct sunlight or tanning beds.

Basic Concept - Ch 75

Burn Resuscitation Priorities

A1Unit 11Airway first

Use this when a major-burn question asks what to assess or do first during the emergent/resuscitative phase.

Related Content

  • Emergent burn priorities include airway protection, circulation/organ perfusion through fluid replacement, pain control, infection prevention, temperature support, and emotional support.
  • Inhalation injury cues include singed hair, soot, hoarseness, wheezing, brassy cough, drooling, difficulty swallowing, crowing, or stridor.

Underlying Principles

  • Initial fluid shift can cause hemoconcentration, elevated BUN, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, and hypothermia risk.
  • Urine output below expected range after a major burn points toward inadequate fluid replacement rather than a need for diuretics.

Nursing Interventions

  • Stop the burning process, remove heat-conducting clothing or jewelry, cool with water rather than ice, cover with a clean cloth, provide warmth, and complete the ABCDE survey.
  • Use IV routes for analgesia during resuscitation because other routes can absorb poorly.

Therapeutic Procedure - Ch 75

Burn Graft, Infection, and Mobility Follow-Up

A13Unit 11Graft protection

Use this when a burn client has wound care, grafting, donor-site pain, infection cues, or contracture-prevention needs.

Description

  • Autografts use the client's own skin; sheet grafts cover wounds, while mesh grafts stretch over larger areas.
  • Synthetic or biosynthetic coverings can support wound visualization, exudate drainage, pain reduction, or healing of selected partial-thickness/donor sites.

Nursing Actions

  • Premedicate before wound care, assess odor/drainage/eschar/bleeding/new skin, cleanse thoroughly, and assist with ordered debridement.
  • After grafting, immobilize graft sites, elevate extremities, provide donor-site care and analgesia, and monitor before and after coverings or grafts for infection.

Complications

  • Report infection cues such as discoloration around the burn, green subcutaneous fat, degeneration of granulation tissue, subeschar hemorrhage, hyperventilation, or unstable temperature.
  • Prevent contractures with active or passive range-of-motion exercises, neutral positioning, splints, early ambulation, and compression dressings as prescribed.

Immunization Screening and Follow-Up

A1Unit 13Contraindications

Use this when a vaccine question asks whether protection is active or passive, which product is safe, or what to document after administration.

Related Content

  • Vaccines stimulate active immunity; immune globulin supplies temporary passive protection after exposure.
  • Live-virus products require careful screening for pregnancy and immune compromise.

Underlying Principles

  • Varicella and MMR are avoided during pregnancy; live attenuated influenza is avoided in pregnancy and immune compromise.
  • Inactivated influenza vaccine is the option used when influenza protection is needed during pregnancy.

Nursing Interventions

  • Screen for anaphylaxis history, pregnancy, severe illness, immune compromise, and product-specific allergy before administration.
  • Provide the vaccine information sheet and document date, route, site, type, manufacturer, lot number, expiration date, and administering clinician/facility information.

Diagnostic Procedure - Ch 86

HIV Confirmation and Monitoring Tests

A3Unit 13Testing sequence

Use this when an HIV question asks which tests confirm infection and which tests monitor stage, progression, or treatment response.

Description

  • Positive HIV antibody screening is confirmed with supplemental antibody testing such as Western blot or indirect immunofluorescence assay.
  • CD4+ count helps classify disease stage; HIV RNA quantification tracks viral load before treatment and during therapy.

Indications

  • All pregnant clients should be screened for HIV.
  • Resistance testing helps guide medication changes when resistance is suspected or CD4+ counts fall despite therapy.

Interpretation

  • A positive confirmatory test means HIV infection; it does not automatically mean the client has AIDS.
  • Falling CD4+ counts or increasing viral load requires follow-up for disease progression, adherence, or resistance.

Antiretroviral Adherence Safety

A7Unit 13Resistance prevention

Use this when a client taking HIV medications needs teaching about dose timing, lab monitoring, or infection reporting.

Purpose

  • Combination antiretroviral therapy uses multiple agents to reduce medication resistance, adverse effects, and dosing burden.
  • Therapy is monitored with CD4+ trends, viral load, CBC, WBC, and liver-function-related results.

Safety Monitoring

  • Missed doses can lead to drug resistance, so schedule adherence is a priority teaching point.

Client Education

  • Use infection-prevention routines, safe-sex practices, nutrition support, and prompt symptom reporting as part of the medication plan.

Therapeutic Procedure - Ch 68

Arthroplasty Positioning and Safety

A13Unit 10Joint protection

Use this when a hip or knee arthroplasty question asks which precautions prevent dislocation, VTE, infection, or neurovascular compromise.

Description

  • Total knee arthroplasty replaces distal femur, tibial plate, and patellar components; total hip arthroplasty replaces the acetabular cup, femoral head, and femoral stem.
  • Recent or active infection, unstable comorbidities, advanced osteoporosis, or inability to follow the postsurgical plan can delay surgery.

Nursing Actions

  • Monitor movement, sensation, color, pulse, and capillary refill of the surgical extremity every 2 to 4 hr and compare with the other side.
  • Assess for bleeding, hypovolemia, excessive drain output, infection, VTE, and prescribed mobility or weight-bearing limits.

Teaching

  • After knee arthroplasty, keep the knee extended and avoid kneeling or deep knee bends.
  • After hip arthroplasty, avoid hip flexion beyond 90 degrees, use a straight-backed chair and raised toilet seat, and follow prescribed assistive-device precautions.

Enoxaparin After Arthroplasty

A7Unit 10VTE prevention

Use this when a postoperative orthopedic medication question asks how low-molecular-weight heparin prevents harm.

Purpose

  • Enoxaparin is an anticoagulant used after orthopedic surgery to reduce DVT risk that can lead to pulmonary embolism.
  • It works alongside early ambulation and prescribed compression or anti-embolism devices.

  • Continue prescribed mobility and VTE-prevention measures after discharge.

Osteoporosis Fracture Prevention

A1Unit 10Fall safety

Use this when an osteoporosis question asks which cues, diagnostics, medication teaching, and home changes prevent fractures.

Related Content

  • Osteoporosis occurs when bone resorption exceeds formation, causing low bone density and fracture risk.
  • Expected cues include height loss, kyphosis, back pain after lifting or bending, restricted movement, spinal deformity, and fracture history.

Underlying Principles

  • DXA screens for early bone-density changes, usually at the hip or spine.
  • Prevention emphasizes calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, smoking cessation, limiting alcohol/caffeine, and fall reduction.

Nursing Interventions

  • Teach oral bisphosphonate clients to protect the esophagus by following strict administration and upright-position instructions.
  • After vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty, anticipate supine positioning immediately after the procedure and monitor pain and neurovascular status as prescribed.

Diagnostic Procedure - Ch 65

PSA, DRE, and TRUS Screening Order

A3Unit 9PSA first

Use this when a male reproductive diagnostic question asks what to collect first or what follow-up means.

Description

  • PSA is a blood test for a prostate-produced protein and should be drawn before DRE because palpation can raise the value.
  • DRE assesses size, shape, consistency, and nodules; TRUS images the prostate and can guide biopsy after abnormal screening findings.

Indications

  • PSA greater than 4 ng/mL, abnormal DRE, hematuria, urinary retention, weak stream, or elevated prostate-cancer risk requires follow-up.
  • EPCA-2 and biopsy help clarify concern for prostate cancer; TRUS alone creates the image.

Nursing Interventions

  • Teach that elevated PSA can occur with prostate cancer, BPH, prostatitis, or recent UTI and is not a diagnosis by itself.
  • Screen for latex allergy before TRUS and reinforce any prescribed enema preparation.

BPH Obstruction and Medication Safety

A11Unit 9Urinary flow

Use this when a BPH question asks which findings fit obstruction or which medication teaching protects safety.

Pathophysiology

  • BPH is prostate enlargement that obstructs urine outflow in older adult male clients.
  • Urinary stasis can lead to recurrent UTI, hematuria, retention, and possible kidney damage.

Assessment

  • Expected findings include frequency, urgency, hesitancy, incomplete emptying, dribbling, nocturia, weak stream, straining, incontinence, and hematuria.
  • DRE for BPH is typically enlarged and smooth, while cancer concern rises with hard, irregular, or nodular findings.

Medications

  • Tamsulosin relaxes the bladder outlet and prostate; teach slow position changes because orthostatic hypotension and syncope can occur.
  • Finasteride can shrink prostate tissue over months; teach sexual adverse effects, breast-enlargement reporting, and pregnancy exposure precautions.

TURP Continuous Bladder Irrigation Safety

A13Unit 9Catheter patency

Use this when a TURP question asks how to prevent obstruction, interpret drainage, or teach discharge restrictions.

Description

  • TURP removes obstructing prostate tissue through the urethra and is a common surgical treatment for BPH.
  • Postoperative care commonly uses a three-way catheter and continuous bladder irrigation with isotonic solution.

Nursing Actions

  • Adjust irrigation to keep the return pink or lighter; bright-red or ketchup-like output with clots requires prompt response.
  • If outflow drops or obstruction is suspected, stop CBI and irrigate per protocol; notify the surgeon if the clot cannot be dislodged.

Teaching

  • Expect urgency from catheter traction and do not void around the catheter.
  • After discharge, avoid heavy lifting, straining, sexual intercourse, NSAIDs, caffeine, and alcohol as prescribed; rest and increase fluids if bleeding follows activity.

Status Epilepticus Emergency Priorities

A1Unit 2Airway first

Use this when seizure activity is prolonged, repeated, or not returning to baseline between events.

Related Content

  • Status epilepticus is a prolonged seizure or repeated seizure activity that can cause hypoxia and permanent neurologic injury.
  • Common triggers include withdrawal from antiseizure therapy, substance withdrawal, infection, head injury, cerebral edema, and metabolic disturbance.

Underlying Principles

  • The immediate threat is oxygenation and brain injury while the seizure continues.

  • Maintain airway, give oxygen, establish IV access, monitor ECG, pulse oximetry, and ABGs, and call for rapid support.
  • Prepare prescribed IV lorazepam or diazepam, followed by phenytoin or fosphenytoin.

Acute Stroke Sorting and Safety

A11Unit 2Time-sensitive

Use this when stroke findings require rapid sorting between ischemic and hemorrhagic priorities plus early safety planning.

Pathophysiology

  • Ischemic stroke is impaired cerebral blood flow from thrombotic or embolic obstruction.
  • Hemorrhagic stroke involves vessel rupture and can increase intracranial pressure as bleeding expands.

Assessment

  • Noncontrast CT is the rapid first-line test used to help determine stroke type and thrombolytic eligibility.
  • Assess side-specific findings such as aphasia, visual-spatial impairment, neglect, hemiparesis, and hemianopsia.

Nursing Care

  • Protect oxygenation and cerebral perfusion, keep the client NPO until swallowing is evaluated, and maintain fall and aspiration precautions.
  • For eligible ischemic stroke, prepare time-sensitive thrombolytic therapy only after hemorrhage and major bleeding risk are ruled out.

Dysphagia and Aspiration Safety

A9Unit 2Swallow safety

Use this when a client after stroke needs oral intake restarted safely.

Indications

  • Stroke can impair the cranial nerve control needed for safe swallowing.
  • All clients after stroke should be evaluated before oral nutrition and hydration resume.

Steps

  • Assess gag and swallowing; keep the client NPO and request speech-language evaluation if swallowing is unsafe or uncertain.

Safety

  • Position upright, place food on the unaffected side when appropriate, and teach chin tuck/head flexion while swallowing.
  • Do not delegate the first post-stroke feeding to assistive personnel.

Wound Dehiscence and Evisceration

A11Postop complicationDo not reinsert

Use this when the incision opens or abdominal contents protrude after surgery.

Pathophysiology

  • Dehiscence is wound separation; evisceration is protrusion of internal organs through the incision.
  • Risk rises with obesity, diabetes, poor nutrition, infection, coughing without splinting, hematoma, or steroid use.

Assessment

Nursing Care

  • Do not push organs back into the wound.

Related Content

  • Priority complications include airway obstruction, hypovolemia, paralytic ileus, DVT, and wound dehiscence/evisceration.

Sick-Day Diabetes Safety

A1Unit 12DKA prevention

Use this when illness, poor intake, or GI losses create a hyperglycemia and ketone-monitoring risk.

Related Content

  • Illness can raise glucose even when the client is eating less.
  • Sick-day care protects hydration, glucose control, and early DKA detection.

Underlying Principles

  • Continue prescribed insulin or oral hypoglycemic therapy unless the provider gives a different plan.

  • Escalate for moderate or large ketones, ketonuria lasting more than 24 hr, persistent glucose above 250 mg/dL, fever, confusion, rapid breathing, persistent GI losses, inability to tolerate liquids, or prolonged illness.

Medication - Ch 82

Insulin Timing and Mixing Safety

A7Unit 12Hypoglycemia prevention

Use this when a question asks how to safely time mealtime insulin or whether ordered insulins can be combined.

Purpose

  • Insulin therapy replaces basal and prandial insulin needs to control fasting, between-meal, and postprandial glucose.
  • Rapid-acting insulin is coordinated with meal availability to reduce hypoglycemia risk.

Medication Administration

  • Do not mix glargine or detemir with other insulin; administer in separate injections.
  • When mixing a rapid- or short-acting insulin with a longer-acting insulin that can be mixed, draw up the shorter-acting insulin first.

Nursing Interventions

  • Check glucose before insulin and monitor for sweating, weakness, dizziness, confusion, headache, tachycardia, or slurred speech at peak times.

Use this when a question asks whether a finding points to stenosis, regurgitation, late valvular disease, or heart-failure risk from valve dysfunction.

  • Stenosis means the valve opening is narrowed and forward blood flow is impeded.

  • Valvular disease can remain quiet until late progression, so a new murmur or changing activity tolerance matters.
  • Murmur timing and location help identify the valve involved; left-sided damage can cause orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, pulmonary-pressure effects, and reduced cardiac output.

Hypertension Staging and Risk Teaching

A11Unit 4Often silent

Use this when a question asks whether blood-pressure findings meet source staging criteria or which teaching protects target organs.

Definition and Staging

  • Hypertension is systolic pressure at or above 130 mm Hg or diastolic pressure at or above 80 mm Hg on two or more assessments.
  • Elevated blood pressure is systolic 120 to 129 mm Hg with diastolic less than 80; Stage I is systolic 130 to 139 or diastolic 80 to 89; Stage II is systolic at least 140 or diastolic at least 90.

Risk and Organ Protection

  • Essential hypertension has no single known cause; secondary hypertension is linked to conditions such as kidney disease, endocrine disorders, neurologic disorders, pregnancy, or medications.
  • Prolonged or poorly controlled hypertension can damage the heart, brain, eyes, kidneys, and peripheral vessels and can cause left ventricular hypertrophy.

Teaching Priorities

  • Support BMI management, glucose control for diabetes, aerobic exercise, smoking cessation, stress reduction, and caffeine/alcohol limits.
  • Teach less than 2.3 g/day sodium, low saturated/trans fat and cholesterol intake, DASH eating, and salt-substitute caution because many substitutes contain potassium.

Hemodialysis Access and Postprocedure Safety

A13Unit 8Access first

Use this when a hemodialysis question asks what must be protected before, during, or after treatment.

  • Hemodialysis can support clients with AKI, CKD, persistent hyperkalemia, medication or illicit drug toxicity, or hypervolemia that does not respond to diuretics.

A3Unit 8Cloudy outflow

Use this when a peritoneal dialysis question asks which complication pattern fits the outflow, access site, or metabolic findings.

Description

  • Peritoneal dialysis instills hypertonic dialysate into the peritoneal cavity, allows dwell time, then drains fluid that contains waste products.

Fluid Volume Deficit vs Excess

A1Unit 6Sort the pattern

Use this when a question asks whether findings point to dehydration/hypovolemia or fluid overload/hypervolemia.

Related Content

  • Fluid volume deficit is loss of water plus electrolytes with decreased circulating volume.
  • Fluid volume excess is too much water and electrolytes, often from impaired regulation or sodium-driven retention.

Assessment

  • Deficit cues include tachycardia, thready pulse, orthostatic hypotension, oliguria, dry mucous membranes, poor turgor, concentrated urine, elevated hematocrit/BUN, and high urine specific gravity.
  • Excess cues include weight gain, edema, bounding pulse, hypertension, JVD, crackles, dyspnea, dilutional labs, and pulmonary edema risk.

Nursing Interventions

  • For deficit, rehydrate as prescribed, monitor I&O and orthostatic vital signs, trend mentation, and use fall precautions.
  • For excess, monitor daily weight, I&O, lung sounds, edema, sodium/potassium, and maintain prescribed sodium/fluid restrictions.

Severe Hyponatremia Neurologic Safety

A11Unit 6Seizure risk

Use this when low sodium is paired with confusion, seizures, or other neurologic deterioration.

Pathophysiology

  • Hyponatremia reflects sodium loss or water gain, causing water to shift into cells and slowing depolarization.

Potassium Replacement and Hyperkalemia Rescue

A7Unit 6Rhythm first

Use this when potassium therapy or high potassium creates cardiac-rhythm risk.

Purpose

  • Potassium supports nerve impulses, skeletal/respiratory muscle, and cardiac tissue, so small changes can become high-risk.
  • Hypokalemia requires safe replacement; severe hyperkalemia requires therapies that stabilize the heart, shift potassium into cells, or remove potassium.

Safety Monitoring

  • Never give potassium by IV bolus, IM, or subcutaneous route; monitor urine output, cardiac rhythm, respirations, oxygen saturation, bowel sounds, hand grasps, DTRs, and IV-site phlebitis.
  • With hyperkalemia, stop potassium infusions/supplements, maintain IV access, restrict potassium as prescribed, monitor I&O and potassium, and watch for hypokalemia during treatment.

  • Severe hyperkalemia can require calcium gluconate, dextrose with regular insulin, sodium bicarbonate for acidosis, loop diuretics if kidney function is adequate, potassium binders, beta-2 agonists, or dialysis as prescribed.

IBD Flare and Complication Safety

A11Unit 7Escalate early

Use this when a bowel inflammation question asks whether the pattern fits ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, diverticulitis, or a dangerous complication.

Pathophysiology

  • Ulcerative colitis primarily affects the rectum/rectosigmoid colon and can cause bloody, mucus-filled stools and left-lower-quadrant cramping.
